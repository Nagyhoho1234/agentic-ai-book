# Chapter 1: Introduction

## Comprehensive Summary

The maturation of biology into biotechnology and the precise manipulation of biological form and function have accelerated considerably, driven by directed evolution, CRISPR/Cas9 genetic engineering, cloud computing, and artificial intelligence. These advances have elevated biotechnology to a U.S. emerging technology priority critical to national security.

### Historical Context
- In 2019, DoD established biotechnology as an enterprise modernization priority
- The White House placed biotechnology on the U.S. Critical and Emerging Technology list (2024)
- The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated both the risks of emerging infectious diseases and the power of rapid vaccine development
- The release of ChatGPT in 2022 launched an international race in AI capabilities
- Executive Order 14110 on Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Development and Use of AI was issued in 2023

### The Convergence of AI and Life Sciences
Computational approaches to biology have existed for four decades, but the convergence with modern AI (deep learning) is relatively new. Key milestones:
- 2012: Deep learning emerged based on neural networks
- 2021: AlphaFold and RoseTTAFold computationally solved the 50-year protein structure prediction challenge
- 2024: Nobel Prize in Chemistry awarded to David Baker (computational protein design) and Demis Hassabis/John Jumper (protein structure prediction)
- AI biological models have proliferated rapidly across diverse applications with variable performance

### The Dual-Use Challenge
- Biological agents can be misused for nefarious purposes (e.g., 2001 Amerithrax attacks)
- AI, like biotechnology, is a dual-use technology -- beneficial applications and potential for harm
- The experiments and tools that could modify pathogens to be more dangerous are the same tools used for developing countermeasures
- Basic biological research improves understanding in small increments, making it very difficult to decide a priori which information should be classified

### Threats Beyond Intentional Misuse
- Upward of 320,000 viruses in nature could infect humans
- Climate change is expanding the range of disease vectors (arboviruses, malaria)
- Zoonotic spillovers are increasing due to urbanization and habitat alteration
- Natural disease events happen continuously; deliberate and accidental events are rare but could be catastrophic

### Study Scope and Approach
The study was directed by Executive Order 14110, with DoD tasking the National Academies to examine benefits and risks at the intersection of AI and biotechnology. The committee was charged with:

**(A)** Assessing ways AI can increase biosecurity risks, including from generative AI trained on biological data, and making mitigation recommendations;

**(B)** Considering national security implications of biological data and datasets for training generative AI models;

**(C)** Assessing ways AI applied to biology can reduce biosecurity risks, including recommendations on data coordination and high-performance computing.

**Scope limitations:**
- Focus on transmissible biological threats with epidemic/pandemic potential
- General-purpose LLMs (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama) are outside scope
- Scientific LLMs trained/fine-tuned on biological data ARE in scope
- Biological data = biomolecular data (DNA, RNA, protein, omics); NOT health/medical data
- The committee did not access classified information

## Key Findings

1. The rapid parallel expansion of AI and biotechnology capabilities has renewed concerns about convergence posing biosecurity risks while also presenting mitigation opportunities.
2. AI tools are already being used by practitioners for medical countermeasures and pharmaceutical interventions for a wide range of illnesses.
3. The challenge in risk assessment is driven by the expansive definition of AI, the pace of progress in both fields, and the uncertainty about how capable AI biological models actually are.
4. Regulatory and oversight guidelines for AI in life sciences are a relatively new policy space, with EO 14110 and the National Security Memorandum on AI as the primary frameworks.

## Practical Takeaways for Scientists

- The convergence of AI and biology is a relatively new research area (post-2021 in earnest) with many uncertainties
- Most basic biological research falls under fundamental research exclusion rules
- Scientists should be aware of the dual-use nature of AI-enabled biological tools
- The 2018 NASEM *Biodefense in the Age of Synthetic Biology* framework remains relevant and is used as the baseline for assessing AI-related risks
- Three public meetings were held (August-October 2024) gathering input from experts in AI, pathogenesis, MCM development, data resources, and HPC
