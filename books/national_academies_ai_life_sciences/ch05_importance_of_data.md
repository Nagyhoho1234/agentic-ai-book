# Chapter 5: Importance of Data in AI-Enabled Biological Models

## Comprehensive Summary

This chapter argues that AI-enabled biological tools will continue to require data-driven AI methods that learn patterns from large biological datasets, because the underlying rules of biology are generally unknown. Unlike games such as chess or Go where rules are fully known and AI can improve without additional data, biological AI requires trusted high-quality data with robust provenance. The chapter addresses biological datasets for training AI models, infrastructure and governance, the data life cycle and national repositories, protecting data assets, and data-driven vulnerabilities and mitigations.

The committee's recommendations on data derive from three key properties of data-driven AI systems:

1. **Risky data are hard to define:** It is not feasible to a priori identify targeted biological data points that, if excluded from training, would prevent risky predictions while still enabling beneficial ones. The main option is to exclude large swaths of data, which hinders research.
2. **State-of-the-art AI uses all available data:** The most competitive AI systems are built using foundation models trained on enormous volumes of general data. Restricting large swaths of data is more likely to hinder research than prevent misuse.
3. **High-quality data are a strategic national asset:** Data-driven AI requires trusted high-quality data, as demonstrated by protein folding AI (trained on PDB), digital pathology AI (trained on curated clinical datasets), and other breakthroughs.

## Biological Datasets for Training AI Models

### Data Accessibility Spectrum
Data accessibility follows a continuum from freely available and in the public domain to highly classified information:

- Federally funded research data is guided toward deposition in repositories meeting criteria from *Desirable Characteristics of Data Repositories for Federally Funded Research*
- Criteria include openness, privacy/confidentiality, Tribal and national data sovereignty, protection of sensitive data, and appropriate cybersecurity
- Data generated by private entities have no requirement for submission to national repositories
- The **FAIR principles** (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) serve as aspirational guidelines, but FAIR does not mean all data are open or immediately accessible

### Evolution of Biological Data Access Policy at NIH
- **2003:** NIH data-sharing policy required data management plan for grants >$500,000 in direct costs
- **2007:** GWAS policy lowered sharing threshold to $0 -- all data should be public
- **2014:** Genomic Data Sharing (GDS) Policy addressed whole exome and whole genome sequencing
- Both GWAS and GDS policies focused on data from humans and are concerned mainly about human privacy, not biosafety
- NIH has recognized ethical questions regarding sharing of infectious disease data; the Human Virome Project will address ethical, legal, and social implications

## Infrastructure and Governance

### Data Governance Models

**dbGaP (Database of Genotypes and Phenotypes):**
- Federal repository serving only as a data repository
- Data Access Committee composed solely of federal employees determines access
- Not well received by research community because it excluded academic/commercial input from decision-making
- Required review for every request, even from the same investigator

**All of Us Research Program:**
- Cloud-backed analytic workbench where researchers can access and analyze data but cannot take individual-level data out of the environment
- Adopted a "passport model" based on user credentials rather than per-request review
- Requires: institutional contractual agreement, training/examination, data user code of conduct, public description of each study
- Data is structured in tiers (Registered Tier for less sensitive data, Controlled Tier for whole genome sequencing)
- Currently does not allow data to be used to fine-tune or train external foundation models -- the model built within the workbench cannot be taken out
- This sandbox approach could be analogous to an access-controlled version of AlphaFold

## The Data Life Cycle and National Repositories

The research data life cycle includes: plan and design, collect and create, analyze and collaborate, evaluate and archive, share and disseminate, publish and reuse. Storage and management are core to all stages.

### Key Infrastructure Needs
- National repositories (PDB, GenBank, SRA) are foundational for data science and AI research
- Their creation, curation, and preservation require significant long-term investment in laboratory and computing infrastructure
- Federal research funding agencies should fund AI hardware infrastructure so the research community can develop AI tools specifically trained on biological data
- Many researchers lack access to the necessary data, computing, and educational resources

### Computing Infrastructure
- **NAIRR (National AI Research Resource):** Pilot led by NSF, in partnership with 12 federal agencies and 26 nongovernmental entities, bringing together computational, data, software, model, training, and user support resources
- **DOE FASST initiative:** Frontiers in AI for Science, Security and Technology, offering DOE national laboratories' connected computing and data generation infrastructure
- **NIH + Cloud:** Partnered with cloud vendors to host large repositories such as SRA (>50 petabytes of raw sequence data) co-located with cloud computing resources (e.g., Serratus, Logan)
- The United States has led HPC R&D for decades (Supercomputing Top 500 List), and national investments in AI infrastructure will be essential for U.S. competitiveness

## Protecting Data Assets for AI-Enabled Biological R&D

The chapter identifies three layers of the AI-enabled biological R&D ecosystem, each with distinct vulnerabilities:

### The Data Layer
- Key datasets used to train AI for biology (and beyond)
- Highly heterogeneous, decentralized, includes general text and images mined from the broader internet
- Vulnerabilities: data poisoning, corruption, manipulation of training data

### The AI Layer
- Sophisticated forms of AI for biological R&D implemented using open-source software (at least 55% of PubMedCentral-Open Access articles have links to open-source software)
- Vulnerabilities: software supply chain attacks, trojans

### The Application Layer
- AI used across design, build, and test stages
- Includes biological design tools, automation agents, AI data analysts
- Vulnerabilities: prompt injection, adversarial inputs
- Corrupted data can be self-amplifying: corrupted outputs at the application layer feed back into the data layer, potentially leading to model collapse

### Data Provenance
Maintaining data provenance is critical:
- Track by whom and where data were created
- Record all AI tools and software dependencies used to generate data
- Link AI tools to training data
- Creates a complete picture of data history, enabling rejection of compromised data

### Protecting Each Layer

**Protecting the Data Layer (Box 5-1):**
- **Blockchains** for decentralized data provenance with strong guarantees against attacks
- **Fine-grained access control** that is neither too restrictive nor too permissive
- **Privacy-preserving machine learning** including homomorphic encryption for performing ML on data without revealing underlying datasets

**Protecting the AI Software Layer (Box 5-2):**
- **Program analysis** to automatically find vulnerabilities with static and dynamic analysis
- **Automated testing** to check for spurious results and detect bugs in ML packages
- **Signature-based malicious package detection** to classify and detect potentially malicious packages

**Protecting the Application Layer (Box 5-3):**
- **Generative AI red teaming** to characterize dangerous outputs before deployment
- **Digital twins** to test autonomous laboratories and robotic systems without risk of creating dangerous biology
- **Anomaly detection** to learn typical usage patterns and detect deviations

## Data-Driven Vulnerabilities and Mitigations

### Potential Vulnerabilities

**Data Poisoning and Corruption:**
- Modification of training data to manipulate AI model behavior
- Examples: submitting falsified research papers to preprint servers with altered gene-drug interaction data, corrupting protein folding data with false structures
- No guarantee that publicly contributed data has been peer reviewed

**Backdoor Attacks:**
- Adversary introduces hidden trigger during training
- Example: a public vaccine-discovery AI model could contain a backdoor activated by a specific DNA or protein structure sequence
- Difficult to detect because it remains dormant during normal usage

**Jailbreaking Attacks:**
- Bypassing safety mechanisms by rephrasing requests or breaking tasks into smaller benign steps
- Particularly concerning because they exploit interpretive processes rather than altering the model itself

**Model Fine-Tuning and Instruction Manipulation:**
- Adversary fine-tunes a pretrained model with malicious data to produce incorrect outputs
- Could steer models toward designing ineffective therapies, producing misleading information, or generating synthetic pathways for toxic compounds
- Requires nontrivial expertise in both ML and the biological domain, plus access to the training pipeline

### Mitigation Approaches

1. **Continuous Penetration Testing:** Probing AI models for weaknesses by simulating attack scenarios
2. **Robust Training Techniques:** Training with well-curated and trusted datasets with strong data provenance; adversarial training environments to improve resistance
3. **Benchmarking for Robustness and Risk Evaluation:** Standardized benchmarks to assess resistance to data corruption, jailbreaking, and backdoor manipulation
4. **Regular Model Audits:** Both automated techniques and manual expert reviews at regular intervals

### Assessment of Large Language Models

Methods for assessing LLMs include:
- Static single-turn prompts with human rating
- Red teaming with simulated adversarial attacks
- Multiple choice questions with automatic grading
- Uplift studies comparing group performance with and without AI
- Various combinations of the above

**Limitations of current assessments:**
- Multiple-choice benchmarks tend to be simplistic and closed-ended
- Cannot be used to "discover" new model capabilities
- Most biosecurity risk assessments test whether LLMs can answer scientific questions, rather than providing actionable harmful information
- Distinction between general LLMs and domain-specific biological LLMs should be considered in assessments
- The science of model assessments is still nascent and needs more research

## Key Findings and Recommendations

**Recommendation 3-1:** Federal research funding agencies (NIH, ARPA-H, DARPA, NSF, DOE, and others) should fund efforts and infrastructures to:
- Create and steward AI-compatible data and training sets for biological applications that are made publicly available
- Initiate and support public-private partnerships for the generation of training datasets
- Develop frontier AI-enabled biological tools that are specifically trained on biological data and focused on biological capabilities

**Recommendation 3-2:** Federal research agencies that house or fund biological databases should consider these to be strategic assets and concordantly implement robust data provenance mechanisms to maintain the highest data quality. They should provide strong incentives and infrastructure for the standardization, curation, integration, and continuous maintenance of high-quality, publicly accessible biological data at scale.

**Recommendation 3-3:** While a public-private partnership is a reasonable approach to pilot the creation of an AI-ready data resource on government-owned infrastructure, a dedicated organization is central to a sustainable national data stewardship resource. The NAIRR pilot should explore approaches for establishing central repositories for training AI models.

## Practical Takeaways for Scientists

- Treat your biological datasets as strategic assets -- invest in standardization, curation, and FAIR compliance
- Data provenance tracking (who created data, what tools were used, what training data was involved) is becoming essential, not optional
- Be aware of data poisoning risks when using publicly available datasets that have not been peer reviewed for AI training
- Consider sandbox environments (like the All of Us Research Program model) for sensitive biological data and AI tools
- The NAIRR pilot and DOE FASST initiative provide new avenues for accessing compute and data infrastructure
- When evaluating AI models for biological applications, consider both standard benchmarks and domain-specific red teaming approaches
- Privacy-preserving machine learning techniques (e.g., homomorphic encryption) may enable analysis of sensitive viral genome data without revealing underlying sequences
- Maintain awareness that AI models are vulnerable to adversarial attacks -- implement robust training techniques and regular auditing
