# Chapter 11: Writing and AI

## Summary

This chapter confronts the central challenge AI poses to writing instruction: writing is what AI does best, and it is proving virtually impossible to create writing prompts that AI cannot handle at least at a C-grade level (Mills, 2023b). Yet writing remains essential both as a professional skill and as a cognitive process -- a way of thinking, reflecting, and discovering what we believe.

The authors draw on Donald Norman's (1991) concept of "cognitive artifacts" -- tools like paper, pens, and computers that affect human cognition. David Krakauer (2016) distinguishes between "complementary" cognitive artifacts (like Arabic numerals or an abacus, which amplify human ability and make us better even when the tool is absent) and "competitive" cognitive artifacts (like GPS or calculators, which replace human ability and make us worse when the tool is absent). It remains unclear which category AI writing assistance will fall into -- will using AI to write make us better or worse writers?

The chapter presents multiple strategies for preserving the cognitive benefits of writing while acknowledging AI's reality:

**Writing as thinking and reflecting**: Require track changes, version history, or tools like Lex.page that mark AI-generated content. Require rough first drafts that show thinking-in-progress. Have students do some writing in class, in front of you.

**Local, unique, personal, and recent**: Assignments that require applying concepts to personal experience, local contexts, recent events, or unique scenarios are harder for AI to replicate. Examples: "Apply W to a specific local problem," "Defend thesis Y as it applies to your life," "Describe how you won (or lost) an argument at Thanksgiving."

**Ethical dilemmas**: Pose ethical scenarios demanding subjective reasoning and moral judgment -- AI can assist with arguments but centering individual agency makes the human perspective essential.

**Prompts as writing**: Since future writing will be AI-assisted, prompt writing is itself a valuable writing skill. Have students craft, test, and refine prompts as a form of writing instruction.

**AI literacy through writing**: Use writing assignments to teach source evaluation, fact-checking, attribution, and critical analysis of AI output. Have students fact-check AI-generated essays, compare human and AI critiques, create and evaluate AI summaries.

## Key Pedagogical Strategies

- **Preserve writing as thinking**: Design assignments where the cognitive process of writing is the learning, not just the product
- **Make it personal and local**: Topics tied to students' experiences, communities, and current events resist AI completion
- **Teach prompt writing as a skill**: Prompt engineering requires the same clarity, precision, and audience-awareness as traditional writing
- **Require process documentation**: Track changes, drafts, version history, and AI transcripts
- **Balance AI resistance with AI integration**: Some assignments should be AI-free (in-class writing); others should explicitly incorporate AI

## Practical Takeaways for University Faculty

1. Accept that you cannot create AI-proof writing prompts -- instead, focus on making the human contribution visible and valuable
2. Require students to submit rough drafts, outlines, or annotated drafts that show their thinking process
3. Use in-class writing as a calibration tool -- compare students' in-class writing with take-home work
4. Assign topics that require personal experience, local knowledge, or recent events that AI cannot access
5. Have students present their papers briefly in class -- knowing they will discuss their writing publicly discourages AI dependence
6. Treat prompt writing as a legitimate writing skill -- assign prompt crafting, testing, and refinement exercises
7. Use AI to teach source evaluation: give students AI-generated essays to fact-check and annotate
8. Consider interview-based writing assignments where students must listen to real people and write about them
