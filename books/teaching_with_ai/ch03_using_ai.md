# Chapter 3: Using AI

## Summary

This practical chapter teaches faculty how to use AI effectively through better prompting strategies and tool selection. The authors argue that prompt engineering is a critical new literacy -- not just for faculty but for students. The quality of AI output depends enormously on the quality of the input, and learning to communicate effectively with AI is itself a form of clear thinking.

The chapter introduces a structured approach to prompting with key components: **Role** (who should the AI act as), **Task** (what should it do), **Goal** (what is the purpose), **Context** (relevant background information), and **Format** (how should the output be structured). The authors demonstrate how adding each element dramatically improves AI responses. They show that vague prompts like "tell me about the Civil War" produce generic content, while detailed prompts specifying audience, purpose, format, and constraints produce much more useful output.

Specific prompting techniques covered include: chain-of-thought prompting (asking AI to reason step by step), few-shot learning (providing examples of desired output), iterative refinement (building on previous responses), and meta-prompting (asking AI to help you write better prompts). The chapter also covers practical considerations like using system prompts, managing context windows, and when to start a new conversation versus continuing an existing one.

The authors compare major AI tools available (as of late 2023): ChatGPT (GPT-4), Claude, Google's Gemini (formerly Bard), Microsoft Copilot, and specialized tools like Perplexity for research, Consensus for academic papers, and various image generators. They note that different tools excel at different tasks and encourage faculty to experiment with multiple platforms.

## Key Pedagogical Strategies

- **Teach prompting as a literacy skill**: Prompt writing requires clarity of thought, specificity, and understanding of audience -- all transferable communication skills
- **Model AI use transparently**: Show students your prompting process, including failures and iterations
- **Assign prompt engineering exercises**: Have students refine prompts to get better outputs as a way to develop critical thinking
- **Use AI to prepare for teaching**: Generate discussion questions, create examples, anticipate student misconceptions, design rubrics

## Practical Takeaways for University Faculty

1. Always specify a role, task, and context in your prompts for better results
2. Iterate: treat AI conversations as dialogues, not one-shot queries
3. Ask AI to critique its own output -- "What might be wrong with this response?" often yields useful self-correction
4. Use AI to generate multiple versions or perspectives, then curate the best elements
5. Try the same prompt across multiple AI tools to compare strengths
6. Provide AI with your syllabus, rubric, or examples to calibrate its responses to your specific course
7. Prompt engineering is itself a valuable skill to teach students -- it requires precision, context-awareness, and iterative thinking
