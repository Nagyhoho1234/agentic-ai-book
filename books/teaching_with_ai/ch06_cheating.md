# Chapter 6: Cheating

## Summary

This chapter provides a nuanced examination of academic dishonesty in the age of AI, arguing that cheating is not a new problem but that AI fundamentally changes the scale, accessibility, and detectability of academic dishonesty. The authors draw on extensive research to show that cheating has always been widespread: surveys consistently find that 60-70% of college students admit to some form of cheating, and the internet and "contract cheating" services had already made it easier before AI arrived.

The key difference with AI is accessibility and cost. Contract cheating services (essay mills) cost money and required planning. AI cheating is free, instant, and available to everyone with internet access. The chapter cites data showing that one-third of college students used ChatGPT for schoolwork during the 2022-23 academic year, with a significant number not disclosing this use.

The authors present a critical analysis of AI detection tools (Turnitin's AI detection, GPTZero, etc.), arguing that they are fundamentally unreliable. Detection tools produce both false positives (accusing innocent students) and false negatives (missing AI use), and they are biased against non-native English speakers. OpenAI itself discontinued its own AI classifier due to low accuracy. The authors argue that detection should not be the primary response to AI -- it creates an arms race that AI will inevitably win.

Instead, the chapter advocates for understanding why students cheat (time pressure, unclear purpose, low motivation, poor assignment design) and addressing root causes. The authors emphasize that the best deterrent to cheating has always been good assignment design, clear purpose, relationships with students, and transparency about expectations. AI amplifies the need for these strategies but does not change their fundamental importance.

Practical anti-cheating strategies discussed include: requiring process documentation (drafts, version history, AI transcripts), in-class writing, oral examinations, personalized topics, and making AI use part of the assignment rather than a violation.

## Key Pedagogical Strategies

- **Address root causes, not symptoms**: Cheating stems from poor motivation, unclear purpose, and bad assignment design
- **Reduce reliance on detection**: AI detectors are unreliable and biased; do not build policy around them
- **Require process documentation**: Drafts, track changes, version history, and AI conversation transcripts
- **Use in-class writing and oral assessments**: These are harder to fake and provide authentic evidence of learning
- **Make AI use transparent**: Some assignments can explicitly include AI, with the human contribution being clearly identifiable

## Practical Takeaways for University Faculty

1. Do not rely on AI detection tools as primary anti-cheating measures -- they produce unacceptable rates of false positives and false negatives
2. Requiring students to show their process (drafts, version history, tracked changes) is more effective than detection
3. Understand that cheating is often a response to poor assignment design, not moral failure -- fix the assignments
4. Consider oral components (presentations, defenses, interviews) as part of assessment
5. Use Google Docs, Lex.page, or similar tools that track version history to make the writing process visible
6. Personalize assignments with local, unique, or personal elements that AI cannot easily replicate
7. Building trust and relationships with students reduces cheating more than policing does
