# Chapter 9: Feedback and Roleplaying with AI

## Summary

This chapter explores how AI can transform feedback, tutoring, and interactive learning experiences. The authors argue that AI's greatest pedagogical potential may be in providing immediate, specific, and customized feedback -- the qualities research has long identified as essential for effective learning (Chickering & Gamson, 1987) but that are nearly impossible for faculty to deliver consistently to every student.

The chapter begins with the story of Jill Watson, Professor Ashok Goel's AI teaching assistant at Georgia Tech (2016), which answered student questions so effectively that one chatbot was nominated for a teaching award and only 10% of students correctly identified it as a bot. This pre-ChatGPT example demonstrates the potential of AI in educational support roles. The newer Jill Watson combines an expert chatbot with ChatGPT, using the expert system to verify and fact-check ChatGPT's responses before students see them.

The authors provide a detailed **Feedback Prompt Template** with five components: (1) **Role** -- who should the AI act as; (2) **Task** -- what the AI should do (guide, quiz, coach, not do the work); (3) **Goal** -- how the AI should evaluate (against a rubric, for specific criteria); (4) **Relationship** -- how the AI should behave (encouraging, balanced, direct); (5) **Process** -- the workflow (ask for work, assess, provide feedback, ask questions, iterate). The key principle: AI should provide feedback and ask questions, never rewrite the student's work.

The chapter covers multiple AI roles: **AI as feedback tool** (simple prompts like "What would make this essay better?"), **AI as tutor** (scaffolded learning with adaptive questioning), **AI as debate partner** (taking opposing positions), **AI as discussion leader** (facilitating group discussions), **AI as role-player** (historical figures, professionals, stakeholders), and **AI for engagement** (running experiments, generating visualizations, supporting teams). Each role includes detailed prompt examples and practical guidance.

An important section addresses the limitations and risks: AI needs guidance on tone (early studies found AI feedback could be perceived as short or insensitive), students may bypass the pedagogical AI tutor for a more "helpful" one that just gives answers, and faculty should practice these prompts themselves before assigning them to students.

## Key Pedagogical Strategies

- **Design feedback prompts, not just assignments**: Create carefully structured prompts that guide AI to provide feedback in the way you would
- **Use the feedback template**: Role, Task, Goal, Relationship, Process
- **AI as Socratic tutor**: Prompt AI to ask questions rather than provide answers
- **Role-playing for deeper learning**: Have students converse with AI as historical figures, professionals, or stakeholders
- **Scaffold with AI feedback at each stage**: Outline, draft, revision -- AI can provide stage-appropriate feedback

## Practical Takeaways for University Faculty

1. Write a detailed feedback prompt for your course and give it to students as their AI feedback tool -- this is more effective than students prompting AI on their own
2. Specify in the prompt that AI must not do the work -- only ask questions, provide feedback, and suggest improvements
3. Require students to submit AI conversation transcripts along with their final work
4. Use AI for role-playing exercises: students can practice interviews, debate historical figures, or consult with AI "experts"
5. AI feedback levels the playing field -- first-generation students get the same quality feedback as those with professor parents
6. Practice the prompts yourself before deploying them in class -- anticipate how AI might go wrong
7. Use Pi for role-playing (no login required) and Claude for longer documents; different tools suit different pedagogical needs
