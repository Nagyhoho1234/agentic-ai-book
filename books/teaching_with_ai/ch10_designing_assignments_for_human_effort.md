# Chapter 10: Designing Assignments and Assessments for Human Effort

## Summary

This chapter addresses the fundamental question: how do we design assignments that motivate human effort when AI can do much of the work? The authors ground their approach in motivation science, presenting a framework built on three intrinsic human drives: "I care" (purpose), "I can" (self-efficacy), and "I matter" (agency).

**Purpose ("I care")**: Students need to understand why an assignment matters. All creatures have a "salience detector" -- is this activity worthwhile? When a goal feels relevant and meaningful, the work seems easier (Burrow et al., 2016). Faculty must make the value of learning explicit: not just "write an essay" but "develop this skill because it will help you in X way." AI makes this even more critical -- if students see no purpose beyond getting a grade, AI offers an irresistible shortcut.

**Self-efficacy ("I can")**: Students must believe they can succeed. Mastery experiences are critical -- success with one set of problems builds confidence for the next (Bandura, 1997). Video game developers keep players in a "pleasantly frustrated" Goldilocks zone (Gee, 2005). AI can help by providing individualized feedback at the right difficulty level, but it can also undermine self-efficacy if students feel AI is always better.

**Agency ("I matter")**: Students need to feel their contribution matters. Choice, autonomy, and actionable feedback increase motivation. If AI is better at the task, why should a student do it? Faculty must make the value of student voice and ideas visible.

The chapter introduces a practical **Assignment Template** (Table 10.1) that maps these motivators to concrete assignment components: Purpose (why), Task (what/how/when/where/with whom), and Criteria (checklist/rubric/examples). The authors advocate for detailed assignment sheets with "ready-to-submit" criteria, and process checklists that break assignments into timed steps.

Two detailed sample checklists are provided: one for a problem set (75 minutes, with specific time allocations for reading, attempting problems, consulting AI, and reflection) and one for a reading reflection (90 minutes across two sittings, incorporating AI for connections and context). Both demonstrate how to structure AI use within a scaffolded learning process.

## Key Pedagogical Strategies

- **Start with "why"**: Every assignment should clearly explain its purpose and value to the student
- **Design for the Goldilocks zone**: Assignments should be challenging enough to be engaging but achievable enough to build confidence
- **Use process checklists**: Break assignments into timed, sequenced steps that make the process visible
- **Include "ready-to-submit" criteria**: Clear expectations for what constitutes completed, quality work
- **Specify AI use within the process**: Tell students when, how, and why to use AI at specific steps

## Practical Takeaways for University Faculty

1. For every assignment, answer the student's implicit question: "Why should I do this instead of having AI do it?"
2. Create detailed assignment sheets that include purpose, process, and criteria -- the Transparency in Learning and Teaching (TILT) framework is a good model
3. Build checklists with specific time allocations for each step -- this scaffolds the process and makes AI one tool among many
4. Integrate AI use explicitly: "At this step, compare your answer with AI. Whose is better and why?"
5. Focus on process-based grading: grade not just what students produce but how they got there
6. Re-evaluate which skills are truly essential in your discipline given AI -- then articulate clearly why those skills matter
7. Use the "I care, I can, I matter" framework to audit your assignments for motivational design
