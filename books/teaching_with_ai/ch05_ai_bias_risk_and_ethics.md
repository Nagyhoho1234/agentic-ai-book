# Chapter 5: AI Bias, Risk, and Ethics

## Summary

This chapter addresses the serious risks and ethical challenges associated with AI, arguing that faculty must understand these issues both to use AI responsibly themselves and to prepare students to navigate an AI-infused world. The chapter covers four major areas: bias, hallucinations, privacy, and broader ethical concerns.

**Bias** is perhaps the most pervasive concern. AI models inherit and amplify biases present in their training data. The authors provide striking examples: AI-generated images of professionals skew heavily toward white males; language models associate certain names with racial or gender stereotypes; and AI writing detectors are significantly biased against non-native English speakers, flagging their writing as AI-generated at much higher rates (Liang et al., 2023). This last point has direct implications for academic integrity enforcement.

**Hallucinations** -- confident but fabricated outputs -- remain a fundamental limitation. AI can generate plausible-sounding but entirely fictional citations, historical events, or scientific claims. The authors cite research showing that approximately 31% of citations provided by ChatGPT 3.5 were either fake or unverifiable, with only modest improvement in GPT-4 (Bankhead, 2023). This makes fact-checking AI output an essential skill.

**Privacy** concerns include the data students share with AI platforms (which may be used for training), FERPA implications of uploading student work or grades, and the potential for AI to generate personal information. The chapter discusses the ongoing debates about AI training data, copyright, and intellectual property.

**Broader ethical issues** include environmental costs (AI training and inference require enormous energy), labor exploitation (human reviewers who filter harmful content), potential for misuse (deepfakes, misinformation), and existential risk debates. The authors argue that these topics belong in every discipline's curriculum, not just computer science.

## Key Pedagogical Strategies

- **Teach AI literacy as critical thinking**: Understanding bias, hallucinations, and limitations is essential for responsible AI use
- **Assign bias detection exercises**: Have students test AI for bias in their discipline's context
- **Model fact-checking**: Demonstrate how to verify AI-generated claims and citations
- **Integrate ethics across the curriculum**: AI ethics is not just a philosophy or CS topic -- it applies to every field
- **Use AI bias as a teaching tool**: AI's biases can spark productive conversations about systemic bias in society

## Practical Takeaways for University Faculty

1. AI writing detectors are unreliable and biased against non-native English speakers -- use them with extreme caution, if at all
2. Never assume AI citations are real; always verify references independently
3. Be cautious about uploading student data, grades, or personal information to AI platforms
4. Discuss the environmental and labor costs of AI with students -- these are real and often hidden
5. AI bias provides an excellent entry point for discussing systemic bias in any discipline
6. Create assignments that require students to identify and analyze AI bias in their field
7. Stay current on your institution's data privacy policies regarding AI use
