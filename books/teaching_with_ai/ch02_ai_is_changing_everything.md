# Chapter 2: AI Is Changing Everything

## Summary

This chapter examines how AI is transforming the professional landscape and what that means for higher education. The authors argue that AI will not simply eliminate jobs wholesale but will reshape virtually every profession by automating routine cognitive tasks. They cite Goldman Sachs research estimating that AI could automate 25-50% of tasks across many occupations, and McKinsey's prediction that generative AI could add $2.6-4.4 trillion annually to the global economy.

The chapter surveys AI's impact across specific fields: medicine (radiology, diagnostics, drug discovery), law (document review, legal research), writing and journalism, programming (GitHub Copilot increasing developer productivity by 55%), scientific research (protein folding with AlphaFold), finance, and education itself. A key finding: AI-assisted communication had the most equalizing effect on the least skilled workers -- it helped lower-performing employees the most while having minimal impact on top performers (Brynjolfsson et al., 2023).

The authors make a crucial distinction between AI replacing humans and AI augmenting humans. They argue that the jobs of the future will emphasize skills that remain distinctly human: critical thinking, creativity, ethical judgment, interpersonal skills, and the ability to work effectively with AI. This has profound implications for what we teach and how we assess learning. The chapter references AAC&U's essential learning outcomes and NACE career readiness competencies, noting that many of the skills employers already value -- communication, critical thinking, teamwork, leadership -- are precisely the ones AI cannot fully replicate.

The chapter also discusses the equity implications: AI could be a great equalizer (giving everyone access to a "writing tutor" or "research assistant") or could widen gaps if access is unequal. The authors stress that preparing students to work alongside AI is now a core educational responsibility.

## Key Pedagogical Strategies

- **Reframe learning outcomes**: Shift from "students will be able to write an essay" to specifying what kind of writing and thinking, at what level of quality, with what tools
- **Emphasize human-AI collaboration skills**: Prepare students to work with AI, not just despite it
- **Focus on higher-order thinking**: AI handles recall and basic analysis well; prioritize evaluation, synthesis, and creation
- **Teach adaptability**: The specific tools will change; meta-skills (learning how to learn, evaluating AI output) will endure

## Practical Takeaways for University Faculty

1. Review your course learning outcomes -- can AI already achieve them? If so, your outcomes need updating
2. The biggest impact of AI is on routine cognitive work; design assignments that require non-routine thinking
3. AI-assisted communication helps weaker students more than stronger ones -- this has equity implications worth leveraging
4. Students will enter workplaces that expect AI competency; refusing to engage with AI in courses may be doing students a disservice
5. Career readiness now includes AI literacy; consider how your courses contribute to this competency
