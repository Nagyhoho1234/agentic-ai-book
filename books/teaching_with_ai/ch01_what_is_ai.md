# Chapter 1: What Is AI?

## Summary

This opening chapter provides a foundational understanding of artificial intelligence for educators who may not have a technical background. Bowen and Watson trace AI from its origins in the 1950s (the Dartmouth workshop, Turing's ideas) through modern large language models (LLMs). They explain that what we call "AI" today -- particularly generative AI like ChatGPT -- is fundamentally a sophisticated pattern-matching and prediction system, not a thinking entity. LLMs work by predicting the next most probable token (word fragment) based on massive training datasets scraped from the internet.

The chapter distinguishes between narrow AI (specialized systems like chess engines or medical imaging tools) and artificial general intelligence (AGI), which remains hypothetical. It covers key concepts including machine learning, neural networks, deep learning, transformers, and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF). The authors emphasize that AI outputs are probabilistic -- the same prompt can yield different responses -- and that LLMs are essentially "stochastic parrots" that mimic patterns without true understanding.

Important technical details include: context windows (how much text an AI can process at once), temperature settings (controlling randomness vs. determinism), tokens (the units AI processes, roughly 3/4 of a word), and the distinction between training data (static, with a cutoff date) and real-time information. The chapter notes that AI models can "hallucinate" -- producing confident but fabricated information -- because they are optimizing for plausible-sounding output, not truth.

## Key Pedagogical Strategies

- **Start with understanding**: Faculty should experiment with AI before making policy decisions
- **Demystify AI for students**: Explain that AI predicts probable next words rather than "thinking"
- **Use multiple AI tools**: Different models (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, etc.) have different strengths, training data, and personalities
- **Recognize limitations**: AI lacks real-time information, makes errors with math, and can hallucinate citations

## Practical Takeaways for University Faculty

1. You do not need to be a computer scientist to understand AI well enough to teach with it -- the core concept is pattern prediction
2. AI is not one thing: distinguish between image generators (Midjourney, DALL-E), text generators (ChatGPT, Claude), and specialized tools
3. AI capabilities are improving rapidly; any specific limitation mentioned today may be resolved tomorrow
4. The "temperature" setting matters: lower temperatures produce more consistent/predictable responses; higher temperatures produce more creative/varied ones
5. AI models have knowledge cutoff dates and may not have current information unless given web access
