# Chapter 1: Introduction

## Comprehensive Summary

The introduction frames the study's purpose and scope. The Committee on Foundational Research Gaps and Future Directions for Digital Twins was convened by the National Academies at the request of the National Science Foundation, the Department of Energy, the National Institutes of Health, the Department of Defense, and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration. The committee's charge was to identify foundational mathematical, statistical, and computational research gaps that must be addressed to enable digital twins across a range of application domains.

The committee organized three domain-specific workshops -- in engineering, biomedical sciences, and atmospheric/climate/sustainability sciences -- to gather information on the state of the art and research needs. These workshops and additional briefings provided the evidence base for the report.

The report is structured around a conceptual framework that identifies four key elements of a digital twin ecosystem:

1. **The virtual representation** -- the computational model or set of coupled models
2. **The physical counterpart** -- the real-world system being twinned and its sensing/observing systems
3. **Physical-to-virtual feedback** -- data assimilation, calibration, inverse problems
4. **Virtual-to-physical feedback** -- control, optimization, decision-making, sensor steering

Overarching concerns about verification, validation, and uncertainty quantification (VVUQ); ethics and privacy; and security cut across all four elements.

The committee emphasizes that a digital twin is more than just a simulation or model. The distinguishing features are: (a) dynamic updating with data from the physical counterpart, (b) predictive capability, and (c) the ability to inform decisions that realize value. The bidirectional interaction between virtual and physical is what makes a digital twin fundamentally different from a traditional computational model.

## Key Research Gaps Identified

- The term "digital twin" is used inconsistently across domains, creating confusion about what qualifies as a digital twin versus a simulation or model
- No unified research agenda exists that cuts across application domains
- The mathematical, statistical, and computational foundations needed are largely shared across domains, but research communities operate in silos

## Practical Takeaways for Scientists

- When evaluating whether your work constitutes a "digital twin," check for all three elements: dynamic data updating, predictive capability, and decision support
- The report provides a useful four-element framework (virtual, physical, physical-to-virtual, virtual-to-physical) for auditing the completeness of your digital twin architecture
- Cross-domain learning is explicitly encouraged -- techniques from weather/climate data assimilation may benefit biomedical digital twins and vice versa
