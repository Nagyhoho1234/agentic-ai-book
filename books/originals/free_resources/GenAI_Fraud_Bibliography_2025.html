A bibliography of genAI-fueled research fraud from 2025

by Sharon Kabel | December 23, 2025

Have you ever thought to yourself, “Boy, I hate all of these stupid AI tools and bots and add-ons. I wish I had a bibliography with 50 carefully arranged references, each one showing a different way AI is making everything worse, perhaps with selected quotations from each reference“?

Do I have good news for you.

I’m a research librarian, with the bulk of my experience being in biomedical literature. Here is a simplified, incomplete sketch of how scientific research gets published.

  1. Do the research: I conduct research, such as running a clinical trial to test a new drug, or giving an online survey to pediatric nurses, or analyzing a huge dataset of genomes.
  2. Write it down: I write down my findings, and create visualizations (like bar charts) to more effectively illustrate my points. I also do a literature review, where I search for other papers on my topic, to make sure that my work isn’t redundant.
  3. Share it: I share my findings, by submitting everything above to a conference or a journal (or both!). I include my affiliation (like Harvard Medical School), to demonstrate that I have some education and credentials. My submitted paper goes through peer review, where several experts check all of my work. My paper is published!
  4. Reuse it: My paper is influential. Maybe it gets cited by other researchers in my field. Maybe it changes a clinical practice guideline, i.e. how doctors decide what to recommend to patients. Maybe it gets picked up by a mainstream publication, and everyone decides to throw out their black spatulas based on my paper.

Because I spend all day, and most of the night, reading and analyzing recently published information, I have the dubious opportunity of seeing every step hijacked by generative AI (genAI) and large language models (LLMs), like ChatGPT.

There are documented cases of people using ChatGPT or its compatriots to:

  • write articles and generate images
  • find references for a literature review
  • bulk submit articles to a bunch of conferences and journals
  • conduct peer review
  • bulk cite the paper, to make the paper look more influential

It is not only possible – it’s likely that a not-insignificant amount of new research has never been touched by human hands.

Academic misconduct – like plagiarism or falsifying images – isn’t new. What is new is the scope, speed, and variety of misconduct, thanks to a few bad actors and many genAI tools. I have to see this in action all the time, so I want all of you to suffer with me.

I have collated 50 stories published in the last ~12 months (or so) from scientific journals, magazines, and blogs about the misuse of genAI in research. This is not a comprehensive search, and it is distinctly not impartial. For the use cases listed above, I think using genAI in research is a colossal waste of time for everyone involved, with the only possible benefit being job security for fellow research librarians. (For clarity’s sake, I have removed most of the in-text footnotes from the block quotes.)

Each reference below vividly illustrates either the titanic scope of research fraud currently going on, the creative ways some researchers misuse genAI, or the depressing apathy in response to this fraud. I grouped the references into: hallucinations; paper mills and peer review; creating data, text, and images; impersonating authors, journals, institutions, or metrics; retractions; and bots and crawlers. While I did not strictly follow their taxonomy, I owe many thanks to Leslie MicIntosh’s and Will White’s “Combatting Truth Decay: A Taxonomy of Disinformation.” Their article and taxonomy were invaluable in helping me to organize my thoughts.

1-6: Hallucinations

If you ask an LLM to find 10 peer-reviewed articles on night shift work and cancer prevalence, ChatGPT or Claude or Grok will comply by giving you an impressive list of journal article citations. At least some of them will be completely made-up. These hallucinations, once invented, are hard to expunge from the world, and reference librarians have to spend an increasing amount of their time looking for something that doesn’t exist. These hallucinations have real-world consequences. In one example I cite below, a genAI ‘scribe’ in a doctor’s office invented a diabetes diagnosis for a patient, which was entered into the patient’s medical record.

“OpenAI’s ChatGPT, Google’s Gemini, Microsoft’s Copilot and other models are befuddling students, researchers and archivists by generating “incorrect or fabricated archival references,” according to the ICRC, which runs some of the world’s most used research archives. (Scientific American has asked the owners of those AI models to comment.) AI models not only point some users to false sources but also cause problems for researchers and librarians, who end up wasting their time looking for requested nonexistent records, says Library of Virginia chief of researcher engagement Sarah Falls. Her library estimates that 15 percent of emailed reference questions it receives are now ChatGPT-generated, and some include hallucinated citations for both published works and unique primary source documents. “For our staff, it is much harder to prove that a unique record doesn’t exist,” she says.”

Vergano, Dan. “AI Slop Is Spurring Record Requests for Imaginary Journals.” Scientific American, December 8, 2025. https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/ai-slop-is-spurring-record-requests-for-imaginary-journals/.

“On Nov. 1, Jeff Hancock, a well-known and oft-cited researcher who leads the Bay Area school’s Social Media Lab, filed an expert declaration in a Minnesota court case over the state’s new ban on political deepfakes. Republicans have sued to block the ban, arguing it’s an unconstitutional limit on free speech. Hancock defended the law in his declaration, explaining how artificial intelligence makes it easier to fabricate videos and discussed deepfakes’ psychological impacts. But he seems to have made an ironic mistake. Hancock cited 15 references in his declaration, mostly research papers related to political deepfakes and their impacts. Two of the 15 sources do not appear to exist.”

To produce a draft note, AI scribes first create an audio re- cording and a transcript of the patient–clinician conversation. This development is remarkable: for the first time, clinical conversations are being recorded on a broad scale. Because of concerns about medical malpractice liability, however, many U.S. health care institutions are deleting transcripts after notes are finalized and aren’t receiving audio recordings from AI scribe systems at all. We believe this destruction of millions of clinical visit transcripts is unacceptable. It prevents meaningful evaluation of AI scribes, and clinical research and quality- improvement initiatives are limited to using flattened summary notes rather than the primary data of patients’ own words. Moreover, whereas clinical and research stakeholders currently lack access to detailed transcript data, AI companies are receiving valuable dialogue to train the next generation of conversational clinical AI tools. “As we and others have observed, some authors of research papers have a bad habit of cutting corners when they compile references for their articles. Rather than dig up original citations, they simply copy reference lists from other sources (in other words, practice hearsay). The result: unreliable, retracted, or even phantom papers persist in the literature. In just one instance of which we’re aware, a made-up article in a made-up journal received nearly 400 citations.”

In addition, although both ChatGPT and Google Gemini provided citations for most answers, the accuracy and legitimacy of these references remained unclear. The absence of traceable evidence makes it difficult for patients and clinicians to confirm guideline concordance. These chatbots tend to fabricate or falsify information and lack citations. This allows easier fabrication of data. The Office of Research Integrity of the US Department of Health and Human Services defines fabrication as the creation of data, whereas falsification is the manipulation of research materials resulting in inaccurate representation. The literature refers to this phenomenon in AL models as “hallucinations”. Athaluri et al. found that among 178 references cited by ChatGPT, 69 lacked a DOI, and 28 of those were determined to be entirely fabricated. Another study conducted by Else23 asked ChatGPT to generate 50 artificial abstracts for review by medical researchers, who identified 32% of these as real.

In our study, ChatGPT and Google Gemini’s information sources were unclear, potentially disconnecting patients from sources and hindering research. The two models were asked to list the resources used to generate the information. ChatGPT provided a total of 114 references, of which 42 were either fabricated or inaccurate, resulting in a 36.8% inaccuracy rate. Conversely, when Google Gemini was prompted to share the resources it used, it responded, “I apologize that I cannot directly fulfill your request for a traditional reference page from my own ‘sources’, but I hope the explanation and the suggestions for reputable organizations are helpful for your research.” and supplied links to home pages such as The Mayo Clinic and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists.

West, Madeline, Amir Alsaidi, Rohail Siddiqi, et al. “Artificial Intelligence in Obstetrics and Gynecology: Evaluating ChatGPT and Google Gemini in Answering Patient Questions.” International Journal of Gynaecology and Obstetrics: The Official Organ of the International Federation of Gynaecology and Obstetrics, ahead of print, October 28, 2025. https://doi.org/10.1002/ijgo.70622.

7-19: Paper mills and peer review

GenAI has made it easy to commit fraud at scale, to the point where journals and conferences have to completely rehaul what articles they accept – if any. One conference received 70% more submissions than it was expecting, likely from authors using genAI to multiply the number of articles they ‘wrote’. In response, some peer reviewers used genAI in order to get through all the submissions quickly, resulting in peer review feedback with hallucinated references and irrelevant comments.

“Conferences including NeurIPS are being overwhelmed with increasing numbers of submissions: NeurIPS fielded 21,575 papers this year, up from under 10,000 in 2020. Another top AI conference, the International Conference on Learning Representations (ICLR), reported a 70% increase in its yearly submissions for 2026’s conference, nearly 20,000 papers, up from just over 11,000 for the 2025 conference. The problem is bigger than a flood of papers at NeurIPS. ICLR’s reviewers used AI to review a large volume submissions – resulting in apparently hallucinated citations and feedback that was “very verbose with lots of bullet points”, according to a recent article in Nature. […] Meanwhile, says Farid, major tech companies and small AI safety organisations now dump their work on arXiv, a site once reserved for little-viewed preprints of math and physics papers, flooding the internet with work that is presented as science – but is not subject to review standards. The cost of this, says Farid, is that it is almost impossible to know what’s actually going on in AI – for journalists, the public, and even experts in the field: “You have no chance, no chance as an average reader to try to understand what is going on in the scientific literature. Your signal-to-noise ratio is basically one. I can barely go to these conferences and figure out what the hell is going on.”

Down, Aisha. “Artificial Intelligence Research Has a Slop Problem, Academics Say: ‘It’s a Mess.’” The Guardian, December 6, 2025. https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2025/dec/06/ai-research-papers.

20-32: Creating data, text, and images

Arguably the easiest and most common use for genAI in research is while writing the paper. While some authors may use genAI for light editing, some may rely heavily or completely on genAI to write entire paragraphs and/or generate images, with outrageous outcomes. Some authors may disclose genAI use, but many – probably most either don’t disclose, or only partly admit how much they used genAI. This particular use of genAI has sparked urgent discussions about what authorship really means.

“From 1 January to 30 June, the team found, 36% of the abstracts in 7177 manuscripts submitted to [the American Association for Cancer Research] contained at least some AI-generated text. But when asked in an automatic step in the submission process to disclose any use of AI to prepare the manuscript, authors only did so for 9% of the papers studied. […] AACR is considering next steps in response to Evanko’s findings, including using the new tool to screen all submissions. But with more than 2500 AACR submissions flagged for AI-generated abstracts from January to June alone, “It’s too many to put a human in the loop” to follow up on each undisclosed instance, he says.”

Brainard, Jeffrey. “Far More Authors Use AI to Write Science Papers than Admit It, Publisher Reports.” Science 389, no. 6766 (2025): 1168–69. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.z87syeh.

33-38: Impersonating authors, journals, institutions, or metrics

Impersonation is one of the trickiest types of fraud to identify at this point. If someone claims they are Dr. Clarence Threepwood from Blandings University publishing in the Journal of Porcine Studies (JOPS):

– is the author’s name really Clarence Threepwood? If he’s not Threepwood, is that a fake name, or the name of a real researcher and this con man is piggybacking off of his name?
– does the author really work for Blandings University (BU)? Is BU a real institution? Is it a ‘mirror’ or copycat institution? Maybe BU doesn’t exist, but Blandings College does.
– is the Journal of Porcine Studies a real journal, or a racket run by Threepwood’s friends so they can all publish each other’s papers?
– has this article really been cited by 10 other papers, or is there a ‘citation ring,’ of Threepwood’s friends all citing each other’s papers to boost visibility and credibility?

39-45: Retractions

Retractions seem simple: a journal article is withdrawn, because of serious mistakes or fraud. However, every publisher has a different way of actually withdrawing the article. Some delete the article from their website; some keep the article up, but put “RETRACTED” on every page; some delete the article, and replace it with a retraction notice. There is also no automatic way to be informed that an article has been retracted. Is every Twitter account, blog writer, and Wikipedia editor checking every single article they link to – not just once, but routinely? Many, if not most, retractions occur years after the initial publication. When LLMs like ChatGPT bother to provide real references, those LLMs are notorious for not identifying which of those references have been edited or retracted.

46-50: Bots and crawlers

We all rely on search engines, and those search engines rely on bots that ‘crawl’ all of the web pages they can get their hands on. GenAI web crawlers not only violate every copyright law known to man by stealing and reusing oceans of proprietary books, journals, etc., but their web crawling is so intense that it can knock entire websites offline. Library catalogs, museums, scholarly journals, newspaper collections, cultural heritage organizations, will all need to consider a) keeping their content available online and risking attacks or thefts, or b) removing everyone’s access entirely. Bots may also be used to flood online surveys, a type of research data many disciplines rely on. In every article I read about surveys and bots, the survey runners decided to cancel their survey entirely because the data was rendered useless by genAI bots.

However, unlike a traditional DDoS attack, they are not intended to knock the site offline. Instead, the site being knocked offline is a side effect of harvesting the data at scale. These incidents tend to last for relatively short periods of time (measured in minutes, maybe an hour or two). Then the swarm moves on. […] The swarms tend to overload servers. That results in slower response times for normal users. Eventually, the volume will knock the server offline entirely. […] I worry that bots will drive up costs for hosting current collections. It may also make institutions reluctant to create new open access and digitally accessible collections.”
