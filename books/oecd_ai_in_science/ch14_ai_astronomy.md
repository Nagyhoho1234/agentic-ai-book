# AI for Astronomy and Astrophysics

**Authors:** Multiple contributors
**Pages:** 137-144

## Summary

This essay surveys AI applications in astronomy and astrophysics, a field that is increasingly characterised by massive datasets from ground- and space-based telescopes. AI is used for galaxy classification, transient detection (supernovae, gravitational wave events), exoplanet identification, and cosmological parameter estimation.

Key examples include: citizen science projects like Galaxy Zoo, which have been augmented or replaced by deep learning classifiers; automated pipelines for detecting transient events in real-time survey data; neural network-based inference of cosmological parameters from simulations; and AI-assisted analysis of gravitational wave data from LIGO/Virgo.

The essay notes that astronomy has traditionally been an early adopter of computational tools and that AI is a natural extension of this tradition. However, challenges include the need for uncertainty quantification (astronomers need to know how confident an AI prediction is), domain adaptation (models trained on one survey may not transfer well to another), and the interpretability of AI-generated results.

## Key Policy Recommendations

- Funding for next-generation astronomical surveys (e.g., Square Kilometre Array, Vera Rubin Observatory) should include dedicated AI/ML components.
- Open data policies for astronomical surveys should be maintained and strengthened.
- Interdisciplinary training in AI for astronomers should be supported.

## Practical Takeaways for Scientists and Institutions

- AI classification tools can process astronomical survey data far faster than human experts, enabling real-time discovery.
- Uncertainty quantification in AI predictions is critical for scientific credibility in astronomy.
- Pre-trained models and transfer learning can help astronomers adapt AI tools from one survey or instrument to another.

## Notable Data and Statistics

- The Square Kilometre Array radio telescope will generate more data per day than the entire Internet.
- Next-generation surveys will detect billions of astronomical objects, far beyond the capacity of human classification.
- Galaxy Zoo demonstrated that citizen scientists could classify galaxies at scale, but AI now matches or exceeds this performance.
