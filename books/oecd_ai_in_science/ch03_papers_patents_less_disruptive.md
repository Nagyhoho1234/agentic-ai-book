# Papers and Patents Are Becoming Less Disruptive Over Time

**Authors:** M. Park, E. Leahey, R. Funk (University of Minnesota / University of Arizona)
**Pages:** 57-64

## Summary

This essay presents evidence based on analysis of 45 million papers and 3.9 million patents that both scientific papers and patents have become less disruptive over the last several decades. "Disruption" is measured using the CD index, which captures whether subsequent work cites a paper's references (consolidating) or only the paper itself (disrupting). The finding is that the proportion of highly disruptive papers and patents has declined substantially since the 1940s across all major fields.

The decline in disruptiveness is not explained by changes in citation practices, team size, or the quality of individual researchers. Instead, the authors suggest it reflects a deeper structural shift: as the stock of scientific knowledge grows, new contributions are more likely to build incrementally on existing work rather than challenge or replace it. The "burden of knowledge" means researchers must spend more time learning what is already known before they can contribute something new.

## Key Policy Recommendations

- Research funding should not only reward novelty but also recognise the value of consolidating and synthesising existing knowledge.
- AI tools that help researchers navigate and synthesise the growing body of scientific literature could help reverse the decline in disruptive ideas.
- Incentive structures that reward only "breakthrough" research may be counterproductive if they ignore the reality of how science progresses.

## Practical Takeaways for Scientists and Institutions

- The declining rate of disruption is a systemic phenomenon, not a failure of individual researchers.
- Tools that reduce the time spent on literature review and knowledge synthesis could free researchers to focus on more creative, potentially disruptive work.
- Institutions should value knowledge synthesis and integrative work, not just novelty.

## Notable Data and Statistics

- Analysis covers 45 million papers (1945-2010) and 3.9 million patents (1976-2010).
- The proportion of highly disruptive papers declined by more than 90% between the 1940s and the 2010s in some fields.
- The decline is observed across all major fields of science, technology, and social science.
- Large teams produce less disruptive work on average than small teams or individual researchers.
