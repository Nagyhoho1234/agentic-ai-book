# AI in Particle Physics

**Authors:** Multiple contributors
**Pages:** 129-136

## Summary

This essay describes how AI and machine learning have become essential tools in particle physics, a field that generates enormous volumes of data from detectors at facilities like the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN. The LHC produces approximately 1 petabyte of data per second during collisions, of which only a tiny fraction can be stored and analysed. AI is critical for real-time event filtering (triggering), event classification, and the search for new physics beyond the Standard Model.

Key applications include: neural network-based jet classification (distinguishing different types of particle interactions), anomaly detection for discovering unexpected new particles or interactions, fast simulation of detector responses using generative models, and optimisation of experimental design.

The essay notes that particle physics has a long history of adopting computational tools and that AI represents a natural evolution of this tradition. However, challenges remain, including the need for interpretable AI models (physicists need to understand why a model makes a particular classification) and the difficulty of using AI for theory development rather than just data analysis.

## Key Policy Recommendations

- Continued investment in computing infrastructure for particle physics is essential, given the data volumes involved.
- AI research applied to physics problems should be supported as a distinct funding category.
- International collaboration on AI tools for particle physics should be encouraged.

## Practical Takeaways for Scientists and Institutions

- AI-based event selection and classification are now standard tools in experimental particle physics.
- Generative models for detector simulation can save enormous amounts of computing time.
- Physicists should develop AI literacy as a core competence, alongside traditional skills in mathematics and experimental methods.

## Notable Data and Statistics

- The LHC generates approximately 1 petabyte of data per second during collisions.
- Only a tiny fraction (approximately 1 in 10 million events) is saved for detailed analysis.
- AI-based triggering systems must process events in real time, making decisions in microseconds.
- Planned LHC upgrades will increase data volumes by an order of magnitude.
