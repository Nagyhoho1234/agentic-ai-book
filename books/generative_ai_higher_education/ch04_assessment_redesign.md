# Chapter 4: Assessment Redesign in the Age of AI

## Summary

This is arguably the most practically important chapter of the book. It addresses the central challenge that GenAI poses to higher education: when AI can produce competent essays, solve problems, and generate code, how do we assess student learning authentically? The authors present the **SARPS framework (Six Assessment Redesign Pivotal Strategies)** as their primary contribution, along with detailed guidance on redesigning assessments for the AI era.

### The Assessment Crisis

The chapter opens by documenting the crisis GenAI has created for traditional assessment:

- **Take-home essays** can be generated by ChatGPT in seconds
- **Multiple-choice exams** can be answered by AI with high accuracy
- **Coding assignments** can be completed by GitHub Copilot
- **Literature reviews** can be drafted by AI tools
- **Problem sets** in many disciplines can be solved by AI

The authors argue that this crisis is actually an opportunity to move toward more authentic, meaningful assessment that better measures real learning.

### GenAI Text Detection -- Why It Fails

The chapter provides a critical analysis of AI detection tools:

- **Detection tools are unreliable** -- they produce false positives (flagging human work as AI-generated) and false negatives (missing AI-generated work)
- **Perplexity-based detection** can be gamed by simple paraphrasing or mixing human and AI text
- **The arms race is unwinnable** -- as AI improves, detection becomes harder
- **False accusations** can have devastating consequences for students
- **Non-native English speakers** are disproportionately flagged by detection tools

The authors strongly caution against relying on AI detection tools as the primary response to GenAI in assessment.

### The SARPS Framework: Six Assessment Redesign Pivotal Strategies

This is the chapter's central framework. Each strategy represents a fundamentally different approach to assessment that is more resistant to AI misuse while also being more authentic and pedagogically sound.

#### Strategy 1: Integrate AI as a Legitimate Tool

- **Principle**: Design assessments where AI use is expected and evaluated
- **How it works**: Students use AI tools as part of the assessment, but are assessed on how they direct, evaluate, refine, and apply AI outputs
- **Examples**:
  - Students use ChatGPT to generate a first draft, then critically analyse, improve, and personalise it
  - Students compare outputs from different AI tools and evaluate their quality
  - Students document their prompting process and reflect on AI interactions
- **Assessment focus**: Quality of human judgment, critical evaluation, and value-added beyond AI output

#### Strategy 2: Emphasise Process Over Product

- **Principle**: Assess the learning journey, not just the final deliverable
- **How it works**: Build assessment around documented process, iterative drafts, reflections, and metacognitive activities
- **Examples**:
  - Portfolio assessment with regular check-ins and documented iterations
  - Reflective journals documenting the research and writing process
  - Video or audio recordings of problem-solving processes
  - Lab notebooks showing experimental reasoning
- **Assessment focus**: Evidence of thinking, learning, growth, and engagement with the material

#### Strategy 3: Use Oral and Interactive Assessments

- **Principle**: Face-to-face assessment is inherently AI-resistant
- **How it works**: Use viva voce examinations, presentations, debates, and interactive discussions to assess understanding
- **Examples**:
  - Oral defences of written work (student must explain and defend their submission)
  - Live problem-solving demonstrations
  - Group presentations with individual Q&A
  - Socratic seminars and structured debates
- **Assessment focus**: Depth of understanding, ability to think on feet, communication skills

#### Strategy 4: Design Personalised and Contextual Assessments

- **Principle**: Make assessments specific enough that generic AI outputs are inadequate
- **How it works**: Connect assessments to personal experiences, local contexts, current events, or unique datasets
- **Examples**:
  - Analyses of local organisations, communities, or case studies
  - Reflections connecting course content to personal or professional experience
  - Assessments using unique, instructor-provided datasets
  - Problems set in highly specific, current contexts that AI training data would not cover
- **Assessment focus**: Application of knowledge to specific, authentic contexts

#### Strategy 5: Focus on Higher-Order Thinking

- **Principle**: Assess capabilities that are harder for current AI to replicate
- **How it works**: Design assessments that require analysis, evaluation, synthesis, and creation rather than recall and comprehension
- **Examples**:
  - Critical evaluation of competing theories or approaches
  - Synthesis across multiple sources with original argumentation
  - Creative problem-solving for novel, ill-defined problems
  - Ethical reasoning about complex dilemmas
- **Assessment focus**: Bloom's higher-order thinking skills, original analysis and argumentation

#### Strategy 6: Use Collaborative and Group Assessments

- **Principle**: Assess interpersonal skills and collaborative processes that AI cannot replicate
- **How it works**: Design group projects with individual accountability, peer assessment, and collaborative process documentation
- **Examples**:
  - Team projects with individual reflections and peer evaluations
  - Collaborative research with documented individual contributions
  - Group presentations with individual Q&A
  - Peer review and feedback processes
- **Assessment focus**: Teamwork, communication, negotiation, and individual contribution to collective outcomes

### AI Assessment Integration Framework

Beyond SARPS, the chapter presents a broader framework for integrating AI considerations into assessment design:

1. **Define the purpose** -- What learning outcomes does the assessment measure?
2. **Assess AI impact** -- Can AI complete this assessment? If so, does that undermine the learning outcome?
3. **Select appropriate strategy** -- Choose from SARPS or combine strategies
4. **Design rubrics** -- Create criteria that value human contribution, critical thinking, and process
5. **Communicate expectations** -- Be clear with students about AI use policies for each assessment
6. **Evaluate and iterate** -- Review assessment effectiveness and adjust

### Rubric Design for AI-Era Assessment

The chapter provides guidance on rubric design:

- **Include criteria for AI interaction** -- quality of prompts, critical evaluation of outputs, value added by the student
- **Assess process documentation** -- evidence of thinking, iteration, and reflection
- **Value originality and personal insight** -- connections to personal experience, unique perspectives
- **Evaluate critical thinking** -- analysis of AI limitations, identification of errors or biases
- **Include academic integrity** -- appropriate attribution and transparency about AI use

### Bloom's Taxonomy and Assessment Redesign

The chapter maps SARPS strategies to Bloom's levels:

| Bloom's Level | AI Capability | Assessment Strategy |
|--------------|---------------|-------------------|
| Remember | AI excels | Move away from recall-based assessment |
| Understand | AI performs well | Assess through oral explanation, not written reproduction |
| Apply | AI can do in routine cases | Use novel, context-specific applications |
| Analyse | AI has limitations | Require critical analysis of AI outputs |
| Evaluate | AI struggles with nuance | Design evaluative tasks requiring human judgment |
| Create | AI can generate but not truly create | Assess originality, synthesis, and creative vision |

### SOLO Taxonomy and Assessment

The chapter also references the SOLO (Structure of Observed Learning Outcomes) Taxonomy:
- **Pre-structural**: No understanding
- **Uni-structural**: One relevant aspect
- **Multi-structural**: Several relevant aspects, independently
- **Relational**: Integration and connection of aspects
- **Extended abstract**: Generalisation to new domains

The authors argue that assessments should target relational and extended abstract levels, where AI is weakest.

### Variability in Assessment

The chapter advocates for assessment variety:
- Mix of formative and summative assessment
- Multiple assessment types within a single course
- Both individual and collaborative assessment
- Both AI-inclusive and AI-free assessment contexts
- Both product-focused and process-focused assessment

## Practical Takeaways for University Faculty

1. **Do not rely on AI detection tools** -- they are unreliable and can harm students unfairly
2. **Apply the SARPS framework** -- use a combination of the six strategies for robust assessment
3. **Start with oral components** -- adding a brief viva voce or presentation to existing assessments is the quickest way to add AI resilience
4. **Redesign rubrics** -- include criteria for process, critical thinking, and personal insight, not just correctness
5. **Be transparent** -- clearly communicate your AI use policy for each assessment
6. **Personalise assessments** -- connect to local contexts, current events, or personal experiences
7. **Assess process, not just product** -- require portfolios, drafts, reflective journals, or documented AI interactions
8. **Use Bloom's and SOLO taxonomies** to ensure assessments target higher-order thinking
9. **Design assessments iteratively** -- build in multiple checkpoints rather than a single submission
10. **Embrace formative assessment** -- use low-stakes, frequent assessment to monitor learning progress
11. **Consider discipline-specific needs** -- a coding course and a philosophy course need different approaches
12. **Involve students** -- discuss assessment redesign with students; they often have valuable insights
