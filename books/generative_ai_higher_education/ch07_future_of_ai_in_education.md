# Chapter 7: The Future of Artificial Intelligence in Education

## Summary

This concluding chapter shifts from description and frameworks to prediction. The authors offer their own forecasts about how GenAI will shape society and education over the coming years, while also presenting responses from ChatGPT (GPT-4), Google Bard, and Microsoft Bing Chat when asked the same question about AI's future impact on education. The chapter draws on historical patterns of technology adoption to ground its predictions.

### Historical Patterns of Technology Adoption

The authors compare GenAI adoption to previous technological shifts:

- **Personal computers (1980s-1990s)** -- Initially niche (for "nerds"), became mainstream only after the internet made them broadly useful. Eventually became essential for education and work
- **Smartphones (2010s)** -- Adopted much more rapidly than PCs due to personal utility, portability, and broad appeal. By 2020, 78% of the world's population owned a smartphone
- **GenAI** -- Adoption pattern likely somewhere between PCs and smartphones. Unlike PCs, GenAI does not require supplementary infrastructure (like spreadsheet software). Unlike smartphones, it may not resonate as broadly with less-educated populations

### Predictions on General GenAI Implications

#### GenAI Adoption by General Population
- Primary users will be more educated members of society
- Younger individuals will adopt more quickly
- GenAI will become integrated into everyday tools (word processors, email) similar to spell-checkers
- A wide variety of GenAI tools will be available at zero or low cost
- Measuring GenAI usage and penetration will be challenging due to seamless integration

#### Job Impact
- Significant "drama" in media about jobs, but reality will be more nuanced
- Goldman Sachs estimates AI automation will impact 46% of Office/Admin work, 44% of Legal, 37% of Architecture/Engineering, 36% of Life/Physical/Social Sciences
- GenAI will primarily serve as an assistance tool, not a replacement for workers
- The rise of GenAI will not jeopardise anyone's job per se -- only workers who fail to adapt
- Knowledge-based economy roles will be impacted more than manual labour
- New roles will emerge: "AI Engineers", "Head of Conversational AI", "AI Ethicists", "Human-Machines Teaming Manager", "Digital Detox Therapist", "AI Personality Designers"
- Holistic competencies (teamwork, leadership, critical thinking) will become more valuable alongside technical skills

#### AI Safety -- Adoption Phase
- People will initially trust AI even on controversial topics
- Privacy and data security concerns will not significantly slow adoption (similar to social media patterns)
- People will continue using AI despite privacy risks, possibly preferring embedded/local AI assistants
- Acceptance and usage may serve as a proxy measure for AI alignment

#### AI Safety -- Post-Adoption Phase
- After widespread adoption, companies and governments may attempt to influence public opinion through AI
- Biased AI recommendations (e.g., promoting paying brands) pose manipulation risks
- AI safety and alignment will only be taken seriously when negative impacts become widespread and visible

#### Impact from Governments
- Regulation will be highly varied across regions
- AI and GenAI adoption will not be significantly impacted by regulation
- Regulation will try to balance innovation with risk management
- Future regulations will likely NOT address how AI is trained, how filtering works, or enable easy portability between AI products
- Once large players are established, they will resist regulatory changes to their centralised control

#### AI Development Companies
- Competition among OpenAI, Microsoft, Meta, Google, Anthropic, Mistral AI, and others will intensify
- Continuous funding will drive innovation
- Hardware and training data remain key challenges, but will not significantly slow growth
- Consolidation around a few key providers is likely, though creative start-ups will also thrive
- Copyright lawsuits (e.g., authors suing OpenAI) could have major financial and strategic impact
- Companies are beginning to offer copyright indemnification (Microsoft, Adobe, Google)

### Predictions on GenAI's Implications in Higher Education

#### Pedagogy and Assessment
- Employers will seek AI-literate graduates with foundational understanding and proficiency in GenAI
- AI literacy will be incorporated into undergraduate education
- Universities will adapt curricula to include comprehensive AI and GenAI education
- Changes will happen gradually, initially on an ad-hoc, teacher-by-teacher basis
- Most institutions will eventually fund broader programme-level integration
- Assessment changes will vary highly between institutions and regions
- Some institutions will make minimal changes; others will push toward authentic, innovative, AI-partnered assessment
- Private institutions may adopt more quickly, becoming early differentiators
- Agile institutions will be best positioned to capitalise on AI-related opportunities

#### Research in Higher Education
- GenAI will significantly expand resources for postgraduate students, supervisors, and examiners
- Students can use AI as advisors and mentors for understanding research areas
- AI can assist with summarising papers, formatting references, improving writing clarity
- Supervisors can use AI to review research proposals and theses more efficiently
- Adoption will depend on institutional policies
- Institutions resistant to GenAI adoption will face pressure from stakeholders
- Cross-institutional consistency challenges will arise

#### Drafting Funding Proposals
- GenAI can substantially aid grant proposal writing and review
- AI will not simplify the process itself but will enhance quality and efficiency
- Transparency and auditability remain crucial for publicly funded research

#### Submitting and Reviewing Journal Articles
- Journals are adopting varying standards (Nature allows text generation with conditions; Lancet restricts usage more heavily)
- GenAI use in academic publishing will increase, prompting expansion of journal criteria for AI disclosure
- Tools will be highly used by authors, editors, and reviewers
- The shift will be from emphasis on peer-reviewed, high-impact factor journals toward scientific validation, impact, and reproducibility
- A specialised AI model for research purposes could trigger a significant shift in scientific review
- AI capabilities may eventually identify validity issues in existing peer-reviewed publications

### What Did the AI Chatbots Say?

The chapter includes an interesting experiment: asking ChatGPT (GPT-4), Google Bard, and Microsoft Bing Chat the same question about GenAI's impact on education over 5-10 years.

**ChatGPT's 10 predictions included:** personalised learning, virtual teaching assistants, interactive simulations, global classrooms, continuous assessment, skill gap identification, accessibility and inclusion, lifelong learning, efficiency in administration, ethical and bias concerns.

**Google Bard's predictions included:** personalised learning, more engaging learning experiences, increased access to education, new skills and knowledge demands, AI-powered tutors, assessment tools, and virtual learning environments.

**Microsoft Bing Chat** provided a more nuanced response, citing real sources including UCL's approach, studies from TU Delft, and UNESCO guidance. It noted that 90% of teachers had never had training on using GenAI in schools.

The authors observe that all three AI systems produced reasonable but largely generic predictions, lacking the nuanced, context-specific analysis that human experts can provide.

### Can AI Predict the Future?

The authors conclude with a critical reflection: AI cannot predict the future any better than humans for most events. LLMs predict the next word/token, not actual future events. The authors caution against blind trust in AI, recommending that people use their own judgment and critical thinking alongside AI tools.

### Final Conclusion

The chapter and book conclude with several key messages:
- GenAI will become part of the social norm, like smartphones
- Industry is adapting rapidly; employees who embrace AI will benefit most
- Government regulation will vary but will not fundamentally impede AI adoption
- In university education, the greatest pressure will be ensuring graduates are ready for the AI-enabled job market
- Assessment changes are needed for fairness and alignment with new realities
- Research practices will be most impacted in peer review, where AI tools may shift emphasis from peer opinion to scientific validation
- There will be considerable "drama" as workforce changes unfold
- **AI will NOT be able to predict the future any better than humans** -- this is important to remember

## Practical Takeaways for University Faculty

1. **Prepare for continuous change** -- GenAI capabilities will keep evolving; build adaptability into your teaching practice
2. **Focus on what employers want** -- AI literacy is becoming a desirable qualification; ensure your graduates have it
3. **Anticipate assessment evolution** -- some institutions will revert to proctored exams; others will innovate; know where your institution is heading
4. **Embrace AI for research** -- use GenAI to enhance your own research productivity (literature review, writing, analysis)
5. **Engage with journal policies** -- understand and follow your target journals' AI use policies
6. **Think about grant writing** -- GenAI can help draft and refine proposals, but transparency about its use is essential
7. **Develop holistic competencies** in students -- teamwork, critical thinking, leadership, and ethical reasoning will be more valuable than ever
8. **Watch for equity issues** -- the digital divide may become an "AI divide"
9. **Do not over-trust AI predictions** -- maintain human judgment and critical thinking
10. **Stay engaged with your institution's policy evolution** -- the landscape is changing rapidly and faculty input matters
