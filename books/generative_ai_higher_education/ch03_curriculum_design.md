# Chapter 3: AI and Curriculum Design

## Summary

This chapter addresses how university curricula must evolve to prepare students for a world permeated by GenAI. The authors argue that curriculum design must go beyond simply adding AI topics to existing courses -- it requires fundamental rethinking of what competencies graduates need and how learning experiences should be structured. The chapter provides practical frameworks and strategies for AI-integrated curriculum design across disciplines.

### The Need for Curriculum Transformation

The authors identify several drivers for curriculum change:

1. **Employer expectations are shifting** -- graduates need AI literacy alongside domain expertise
2. **Traditional knowledge-transfer models are insufficient** -- when AI can provide information instantly, higher-order thinking becomes more important
3. **New competencies are needed** -- prompt engineering, AI-assisted research, critical evaluation of AI outputs
4. **Existing competencies need reframing** -- what does "writing" mean when AI can generate text? What does "research" mean when AI can summarise literature?

### Bloom's Taxonomy in the AI Era

The chapter extensively applies Bloom's Taxonomy to AI-integrated curriculum design:

- **Remember/Understand** -- These lower-order tasks are most easily performed by GenAI. Curricula should move away from assessments focused solely on recall and comprehension
- **Apply** -- Students should learn to apply knowledge in contexts where AI is a tool, not a replacement
- **Analyse** -- Critical analysis of AI outputs, comparison of AI-generated vs. human-generated content
- **Evaluate** -- Judging the quality, accuracy, and appropriateness of AI outputs for specific purposes
- **Create** -- Using AI as a co-creative tool while maintaining original thought, synthesis, and creativity

### Curriculum Design Principles

The authors propose several principles:

1. **AI as a pedagogical partner** -- Design learning experiences where AI enhances rather than replaces learning
2. **Scaffolded AI integration** -- Start with structured AI use and gradually give students more autonomy
3. **Discipline-specific approaches** -- Different fields require different AI integration strategies
4. **Process over product** -- Emphasise the learning process, not just the final output
5. **Experiential learning** -- Use AI to create richer, more authentic learning experiences
6. **Reflective practice** -- Build in regular reflection on AI use and its implications

### Prompt Engineering as Curriculum Content

The chapter devotes significant attention to prompt engineering as a teachable skill:

- **Basic prompting** -- Clear instructions, specifying format and length
- **Role-based prompting** -- Assigning the AI a specific role or expertise
- **Chain of thought prompting** -- Asking the AI to reason step by step
- **Few-shot prompting** -- Providing examples of desired outputs
- **Negative prompting** -- Specifying what the AI should NOT do
- **Prompt engineering components** -- Context, instruction, input data, output indicator

The authors argue that prompt engineering is a new form of literacy that develops critical thinking, clear communication, and systematic problem-solving skills.

### AI-Partnered Pedagogy

The chapter introduces the concept of AI-partnered education, where AI tools are deliberately integrated into learning activities:

- **AI as tutor** -- Students use AI for personalised explanations and practice
- **AI as collaborator** -- Students work with AI on projects, learning to direct and evaluate AI contributions
- **AI as critic** -- Students use AI to get feedback on drafts before peer or instructor review
- **AI as simulator** -- Using AI to model scenarios, generate case studies, or create practice problems
- **AI as research assistant** -- Using AI to summarise literature, identify gaps, or generate hypotheses

### Curriculum Design for Different Disciplines

The chapter provides discipline-specific guidance:

- **STEM** -- Focus on using AI for data analysis, code generation, and hypothesis testing while maintaining understanding of underlying principles
- **Humanities** -- Focus on critical analysis of AI-generated text, AI and creativity, ethical implications of AI authorship
- **Social Sciences** -- Focus on AI's impact on society, using AI for qualitative and quantitative research, ethical research with AI
- **Professional programmes** (Law, Medicine, Business) -- Focus on AI as a professional tool, ethical use in practice, regulatory implications
- **Creative Arts** -- Focus on AI as a creative collaborator, questions of authorship and originality

### Experiential and Problem-Based Learning with AI

The chapter advocates for learning approaches that:
- Use real-world problems that AI alone cannot solve
- Require students to combine AI capabilities with human judgment
- Build in collaboration, communication, and teamwork alongside AI use
- Include reflection on the AI-enhanced process

### Soft Skills and AI

The authors emphasise that as AI handles more technical tasks, soft skills become more valuable:
- Critical thinking and judgment
- Communication and interpersonal skills
- Creativity and innovation
- Ethical reasoning
- Teamwork and leadership
- Adaptability and lifelong learning

## Practical Takeaways for University Faculty

1. **Audit your curriculum** -- identify where GenAI makes existing learning outcomes obsolete or insufficient
2. **Redesign learning outcomes** -- shift from knowledge recall toward higher-order thinking, critical evaluation, and creative application
3. **Teach prompt engineering** explicitly -- it develops transferable critical thinking skills
4. **Design AI-inclusive activities** -- create assignments where students use AI as part of the process, documenting and reflecting on their interaction
5. **Maintain disciplinary depth** -- AI integration should enhance, not dilute, domain expertise
6. **Build in scaffolding** -- start with guided AI use and progressively increase student autonomy
7. **Include AI ethics** in every discipline, not just computing courses
8. **Prioritise process documentation** -- have students show their working process, including AI interactions
9. **Collaborate across departments** -- AI literacy is interdisciplinary; share approaches and resources
10. **Plan for continuous updating** -- build flexibility into curricula to accommodate rapidly evolving AI capabilities
