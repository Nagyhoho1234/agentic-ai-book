# Chapter 2: AI Literacy in Higher Education

## Summary

This chapter argues that AI literacy is now an essential competency for all university graduates, not just those in technical fields. The authors present comprehensive frameworks for understanding, developing, and assessing AI literacy across disciplines. The chapter makes a critical distinction between digital literacy (the ability to use digital tools) and AI literacy (the ability to understand, critically evaluate, and effectively interact with AI systems).

### Defining AI Literacy

AI literacy goes beyond knowing how to use ChatGPT. The authors define it as encompassing:

1. **Know and understand AI** -- Understanding what AI is, how it works at a conceptual level, its capabilities and limitations
2. **Use and apply AI** -- Being able to interact with AI tools effectively, including prompt engineering
3. **Evaluate and create AI** -- Critically assessing AI outputs, understanding bias, and potentially creating AI-enhanced solutions
4. **AI ethics** -- Understanding the ethical implications of AI use, including privacy, fairness, transparency, and accountability

### The Dynamic AI Literacy Model (DAILM)

The DAILM framework is a key contribution of this chapter, designed to help educators think about AI literacy across different levels and contexts. It recognises that AI literacy is not static but must evolve as the technology changes. The model maps AI literacy competencies for specific roles:

- **Students**: Need to understand AI capabilities, use AI tools responsibly, evaluate AI outputs critically, and understand ethical implications
- **Faculty**: Need all student competencies plus the ability to design AI-integrated curriculum, assess AI-enhanced work, and model responsible AI use
- **Administrators**: Need strategic understanding of AI's institutional implications

### AI Literacy Framework Components

The chapter presents detailed competency areas:

1. **Technical understanding** -- Not programming, but conceptual understanding of how models work, what training data is, what tokens are, and why hallucinations occur
2. **Practical skills** -- Prompt engineering, evaluating outputs, knowing when AI is and is not appropriate for a task
3. **Critical thinking** -- Assessing AI outputs for accuracy, bias, and relevance; understanding the limitations of AI-generated content
4. **Ethical awareness** -- Privacy implications of using AI tools, data ownership, attribution, academic integrity
5. **Social and emotional intelligence** -- Understanding AI's impact on human relationships, communication, creativity

### Competency Levels

The framework identifies progressive levels:
- **Basic**: Can use AI tools, understands what they are
- **Intermediate**: Can evaluate outputs, adapt prompts, understand limitations
- **Advanced**: Can critically assess AI systems, design AI-integrated workflows, contribute to AI policy discussions

### Information Literacy vs. AI Literacy

The authors draw parallels between the evolution of information literacy (which became essential with the internet) and the current need for AI literacy. Just as students had to learn to evaluate web sources, they now need to learn to evaluate AI outputs. Key differences:
- AI outputs can appear authoritative even when wrong
- AI can generate plausible but fabricated citations
- The line between student work and AI work is blurry
- AI literacy requires understanding of probabilistic rather than deterministic systems

### Media Literacy and AI

The chapter addresses the intersection with media literacy:
- AI-generated deepfakes and misinformation
- The need to verify AI-generated claims
- Understanding how AI can be used for both creation and manipulation of media

### Computational Literacy

The authors distinguish between:
- **Computational thinking** -- Problem decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction
- **Data literacy** -- Understanding data sources, quality, and bias
- **AI literacy** -- Understanding AI-specific concepts like training, inference, and hallucination

## Practical Takeaways for University Faculty

1. **Integrate AI literacy across the curriculum** -- it should not be confined to computer science departments
2. **Start with conceptual understanding** -- students need to understand why ChatGPT sometimes "makes things up" (it predicts tokens, not facts)
3. **Teach prompt engineering** as a practical skill -- better prompts yield better outputs
4. **Develop critical evaluation skills** -- have students fact-check AI outputs as an exercise
5. **Address ethics explicitly** -- discuss data privacy (what happens to prompts?), attribution, and academic integrity
6. **Model AI literacy yourself** -- demonstrate how you use and evaluate AI tools in your own work
7. **Recognise disciplinary differences** -- AI literacy looks different in medicine vs. literature vs. engineering
8. **Make AI literacy assessable** -- include it in learning outcomes and rubrics
9. **Keep updating** -- AI literacy is a moving target; revisit your approach regularly
