# Chapter 8: AI as a Coach

## Summary

This chapter addresses what Mollick considers the biggest danger AI poses to education: not the destruction of homework, but the undermining of the apprenticeship system through which people develop genuine expertise. AI can help solve this by serving as an always-available coach for deliberate practice.

### The Apprenticeship Crisis

For most professional workers, leaving school marks the **beginning**, not the end, of their practical education. Expertise is traditionally built through:
- On-the-job training with experienced mentors
- Apprenticeship (the intern, the medical resident, the junior associate)
- Years of practice under supervision

AI threatens this system because:
- Bosses will prefer AI that "gets the job done fast" over training human juniors
- The tasks traditionally given to beginners (research, drafting, routine analysis) are exactly what AI does well
- If junior workers don't do these tasks, they never develop expertise

**The surgical robot example (Matthew Beane, UC Santa Barbara):** Robotic surgery requires only one operator. In traditional surgery, experienced and trainee surgeons worked side by side. With robots, trainees are reduced to watching. Many ended up "shadow learning" from YouTube rather than hands-on practice. This pattern will spread as AI automates more beginner-level tasks.

### Building Expertise in the Age of AI

**Why facts still matter:** It might seem logical that since AI knows everything, humans don't need to memorize facts. The opposite is true:
- Problem-solving, critical thinking, and evaluating AI output all require a base of factual knowledge stored in long-term memory
- Working memory has 3-5 "slots" and retains new information for less than 30 seconds
- Expert performance depends on a rich network of interconnected knowledge in long-term memory that can be recalled instantly
- Without this foundation, you cannot evaluate whether AI output is correct

**Deliberate practice (Anders Ericsson):** Expertise comes not from repetition but from *deliberate* practice:
- Focused engagement at the edge of current ability
- Immediate, specific feedback
- Continual ratcheting up of difficulty
- Requires a coach or mentor who is themselves an expert
- Much harder and less fun than casual repetition
- The "10,000 hours" popularization is misleading -- it is the *type* of practice, not just the amount

**The Sophie and Naomi analogy:** Two piano students -- Sophie practices the same comfortable pieces repeatedly; Naomi practices under a coach who pushes her to progressively harder material with specific feedback. Despite equal hours, Naomi progresses far faster. This is the difference between repetition and deliberate practice.

### AI as a Deliberate Practice Coach

AI can provide elements of deliberate practice that are usually scarce:
- **Immediate feedback** on every attempt (not once a week from a busy mentor)
- **Adaptive difficulty** that increases as the learner improves
- **Patience** -- never gets tired, never judges, always available
- **Multiple perspectives** -- can simulate different types of feedback

**Wharton pitch simulator example:** Mollick's team built a multi-AI coaching system:
1. An instruction AI teaches pitching principles and answers questions
2. A practice AI simulates a venture capitalist grilling the student
3. A monitoring AI secretly tracks performance data
4. A mentor AI evaluates the practice session and provides feedback

This embodies the structure of deliberate practice -- instruction, practice at the edge of ability, immediate feedback, and reflection.

**Limitations:** Current AI cannot yet fully connect complex concepts, hallucinates too much for some domains, and lacks true understanding of the learner's development trajectory. But these limitations are temporary.

### When Everyone Is an Expert

**The great equalizer effect:** Across every study, AI benefits the lowest performers the most:
- In the BCG study: gap between top and bottom performers shrank from 22% to 4% with GPT-4
- In creative writing: AI "effectively equalizes the creativity scores across less and more creative writers"
- In law: bottom students using AI equalized with top students (who actually saw a slight decline with AI)
- In a call center study: lowest performers became 35% more productive; experienced workers gained very little
- CEO of Turnitin: "Most of our employees are engineers and we have a few hundred of them... in eighteen months we will need twenty percent of them, and we can start hiring them out of high school rather than four-year colleges"

**Will expertise die?** Mollick argues no:
- Jobs consist of many tasks; AI won't automate all of them
- The Jagged Frontier means human judgment remains critical
- AI may enable a new type of expertise: being exceptionally good at working with AI
- Some people may be "kings and queens of AI" -- naturally gifted at Cyborg practices, getting orders of magnitude more value than average users

**The Mollick experience:** Ethan and Lilach Mollick found themselves in high demand as AI educators because they had deep expertise in both pedagogy and prompt design. OpenAI and Microsoft shared their prompts as examples. Their unique combination of game design experience, teaching expertise, and ability to take multiple perspectives (AI, instructor, student) proved unexpectedly valuable.

### The Future of Expertise

- Expertise will matter more, not less -- you need deep knowledge to evaluate AI output
- Students still need to learn facts, writing, critical thinking -- the foundations don't change
- At the same time, everyone's total range of abilities will grow as AI fills gaps
- The future likely involves narrower but deeper human specialization, with AI handling the breadth
- If AI capabilities don't change radically, it truly becomes a co-intelligence -- filling gaps and pushing us to improve

## Key Definitions

- **Deliberate practice (Ericsson):** Structured practice at the edge of ability with immediate expert feedback, as opposed to mere repetition.
- **Working memory:** The brain's limited-capacity processing center (3-5 slots, <30 seconds retention) that draws on long-term memory for problem-solving.
- **Shadow learning:** When trainees learn informally (e.g., from YouTube) because formal training pathways have been disrupted by technology.
- **Kings and queens of AI:** Mollick's term for hypothetical individuals who are exceptionally skilled at working with AI and derive outsized value from it.

## Practical Takeaways for Scientists

1. **Do not outsource foundational knowledge to AI** -- expertise requires deep factual grounding stored in long-term memory. You cannot evaluate AI outputs without it.
2. **Use AI as a practice partner** -- for presentations, grant pitches, manuscript defense, viva voce preparation. Have AI simulate a hostile reviewer or skeptical colleague.
3. **Protect the apprenticeship pipeline** -- if you manage junior researchers, ensure they still do substantive work rather than delegating everything to AI. Their expertise development depends on it.
4. **AI can accelerate expertise development** -- use it for deliberate practice with immediate feedback, but only in areas where you have enough background to evaluate the feedback.
5. **The great equalizer effect means rethinking lab management** -- weaker team members will benefit most from AI tools. Consider how this changes hiring, training, and task allocation.
