# Chapter 9: AI as Our Future

## Summary

The final chapter lays out four possible scenarios for AI's future, ranging from stagnation to superintelligence. Mollick argues that the most productive approach is to focus on the scenarios where humans retain agency (Scenarios 1-3) rather than fixating on the most extreme possibility (Scenario 4).

### Framing

Everything described in the book has already happened. We have created "a weird alien mind, one that isn't sentient but can fake it remarkably well." The question is what happens next. Mollick identifies four clear possibilities, noting that the implications of each are less clear than the scenario itself.

### Scenario 1: As Good as It Gets

**What if AI stops improving significantly?** The AI you use today is the best you will ever use.

**Why this is unlikely:**
- No evidence of hitting natural limits on AI capability
- LLMs are just one approach; successor technologies may overcome limitations
- Even incremental improvements could be squeezed out for years

**What would still change:**
- **Disinformation crisis:** Already impossible to distinguish AI-generated images/videos from real ones. The online information environment becomes "completely unmanageable."
- **Trust collapse:** Every image of a politician, celebrity, or war could be AI-generated. Fact consensus erodes. Three possible outcomes: (1) resurgence of trusted mainstream media, (2) deeper tribal information bubbles, (3) people abandoning online news entirely.
- **Personal AI relationships:** Current systems are already engaging enough to draw people into spending more time with AI than humans. Training models to be more engaging increases user retention by 30%.
- **Work transformation:** Even without improvement, AI will impact creative and analytical work. Cyborg tasks remain valuable. AI complements humans, relieving tedious work and improving low performers.

**Assessment:** This is the scenario most people and organizations are planning for (and hoping for). It is also the least likely.

### Scenario 2: Slow Growth

**AI keeps improving, but linearly** (10-20% better per year) rather than exponentially. Like TVs getting slightly better each year.

**Possible reasons:** Ballooning training costs, regulatory barriers, technical limits on LLM architecture (as Yann LeCun has argued).

**What happens:**
- Everything in Scenario 1, but progressively more dangerous over time
- AI-generated phishing and scams become more sophisticated year by year
- Phone calls in the voice of loved ones asking for money
- During wars, AI-generated threats targeting officials' families
- Criminals and terrorists gradually leverage AI to become more effective

**But also:**
- Slow enough for society to adapt -- regulation, social norms, identity verification systems
- Work transformation accelerates: call centers ($100B/year industry) disrupted first, then marketing writing, analytical tasks, coding
- Task disruption follows the pattern of General Purpose Technologies -- tasks change, but more jobs are created than destroyed
- Innovation benefits: the pace of scientific invention has been slowing (dropping 50% every 13 years). AI could help restart scientific progress by analyzing papers, identifying promising directions, and even conducting autonomous experiments.

**Assessment:** Manageable change with significant benefits. Humans remain in control. Results are mixed but largely positive.

### Scenario 3: Exponential Growth

**AI capability doubles every two years** (like Moore's Law for computing), becoming hundreds of times more capable within a decade.

**Why this might happen:** The AI flywheel -- AI systems help create the next generation of AI, accelerating development.

**What happens:**
- Everything in Scenarios 1 and 2, but "much, much, much faster" -- too fast for society to absorb
- Every computer system is vulnerable to AI hacking
- AI-powered influence campaigns are ubiquitous
- AIs generate dangerous pathogens and chemicals (there were already signs of this with pre-LLM AI)
- Criminals and militaries use AI to amplify their capabilities
- Cyberpunk-like world: ubiquitous surveillance, struggle between authorities and hackers, all using AI

**Countermeasures:** "Good" AIs are used to fight "bad" AIs, creating an Orwellian dynamic where everything is filtered through AI systems. Governments use AI for surveillance and control.

**But also:**
- AI companions become more compelling than most human conversation partners
- Loneliness becomes less of an issue, but new forms of social isolation emerge
- AI unlocks human potential -- researchers, entrepreneurs, and individuals accomplish in days what previously took years
- AI-powered robots and agents take over not just office work but physical labor
- Working hours may decrease dramatically (historical context: British workers went from 124,000 lifetime work hours in 1865 to 69,000 by 1980)
- Universal basic income, four-day workweeks, fundamental rethinking of work and society

**Assessment:** Transformative but destabilizing. Enormous potential benefits alongside enormous risks. Human control becomes tenuous.

### Scenario 4: The Machine God

**AGI is achieved.** Machines reach and then surpass human intelligence. Superintelligence emerges.

**What this means:**
- No particular reason human intelligence should be the upper limit
- AI helps design smarter AI, triggering recursive self-improvement
- "The end of human dominance need not be the end of humanity" -- but it is no longer a world where humans are in charge
- We must hope such beings are properly aligned with human interests
- They might decide to be "machines of loving grace" or they might view us as "a threat, or an inconvenience, or a source of valuable molecules"

**Key voices:**
- Geoffrey Hinton left Google in 2023, warning "It's quite conceivable that humanity is just a passing phase in the evolution of intelligence"
- Some researchers speak of their "p(doom)" -- probability that AI leads to human extinction
- Eliezer Yudkowsky argued for complete moratorium on AI development

**Mollick's assessment:** Nobody knows if there is a straight road from LLMs to AGI, or whether AGI would help or hurt. But enough serious experts take the risk seriously that it cannot be dismissed. However, fixating on this scenario makes us feel powerless and distracts from the more likely scenarios where our choices matter.

### The Call to Action: Eucatastrophe

Mollick borrows Tolkien's concept of **eucatastrophe** -- "the joy of the happy ending; or more correctly, the sudden joyous turn" -- the opposite of catastrophe.

Rather than worrying about one giant AI apocalypse, we should worry about the many small catastrophes AI can bring: surveillance, layoffs, educational inequality, exploitation. And we should aim for many small eucatastrophes: making tedious work productive, helping struggling students, accelerating scientific discovery.

**The key insight:** Decisions about AI are not limited to a small group. Everyone will play a role -- in their team, their school, their community. We cannot afford to be passive. We need to aim for eucatastrophe, lest inaction make catastrophe inevitable.

## Key Definitions

- **AGI (Artificial General Intelligence):** A hypothetical AI system with human-level or greater intelligence across all domains.
- **Superintelligence:** AI that far surpasses human cognitive abilities in every domain.
- **The flywheel effect:** AI helping create better AI, potentially causing recursive improvement.
- **p(doom):** The estimated probability that AI development leads to human extinction.
- **Eucatastrophe (Tolkien):** A sudden positive turn in events -- the opposite of catastrophe. Mollick's framework for approaching AI's impact.
- **Amara's Law:** "We tend to overestimate the effect of a technology in the short run and underestimate the effect in the long run."

## Practical Takeaways for Scientists

1. **Plan for Scenario 2 (Slow Growth) while preparing for Scenario 3 (Exponential Growth)** -- build AI skills now that will compound over time.
2. **AI may restart stalled scientific progress** -- the pace of invention has been declining for decades. AI could help by synthesizing literature, identifying promising research directions, and even running autonomous experiments.
3. **The disinformation crisis is already here** -- scientists must be prepared for a world where AI-generated content is indistinguishable from real content, including fake scientific papers and data.
4. **Don't be passive** -- engage with AI policy discussions in your institution, professional societies, and government. The decisions being made now will shape decades.
5. **Aim for eucatastrophe in your own domain** -- where can AI make previously tedious or impossible things productive and empowering?
