## Comprehensive Summary

This section establishes the ethical and normative foundations upon which AI governance structures and policies must be built. The core conviction is that all governance problems raise value questions, and theories of values deepen our understanding of governance challenges even when the problem itself is not primarily ethical.

### Fairness and Justice (Ch. 6)

Vredenburgh delivers one of the section's most directly applicable analyses. She distinguishes between algorithmic bias as a **statistical** concept (deviation from true state) and as a **moral** concept (encoding wrongfully discriminatory patterns). Bias can enter at every stage of the AI pipeline:

- **Task specification:** How the problem is defined can itself be discriminatory (e.g., predicting total medical expenditure rather than health need disadvantages Black patients)
- **Data collection:** Oversampling, undersampling, measurement error, and data generated by discriminatory institutions
- **Model building:** Choice of optimization metric, proxy attributes that correlate with protected characteristics, accuracy-antidiscrimination tradeoffs
- **Deployment context:** Context shifts, feedback loops, decision thresholds that compound disadvantage

Critically, Vredenburgh argues that the problem of algorithmic bias is fundamentally a problem of **justice, not merely fairness**. Fairness (treating like cases alike) is an "empty" standard without prior justice norms that determine which properties matter. She argues for the **priority of justice over fairness** because:
- Without just institutions, fairness has no moral value
- Fair decision procedures can compound structural injustice when operating on unjust inputs

**Five governance strategies for just AI:**
1. Take a values-first approach to bias interventions (let values drive design, not data availability)
2. De-couple decision processes (avoid using the same AI system across multiple domains, as this compounds injustice)
3. Model structural injustice explicitly in systems design
4. Improve data quality through regulation and public data investment
5. Replace decision thresholds with weighted lotteries where fairness is a priority

### Privacy (Ch. 7)

Veliz writes from moral philosophy, distinguishing between **access** to personal information and **control** over it. Privacy is not merely about preventing individual harm but about securing collective goods such as democracy. "Privacy is about power." Critically, Veliz argues that **consent** plays only a very limited role in securing privacy -- a finding that challenges many current approaches to data governance that rely heavily on consent mechanisms.

### Accountability (Ch. 8)

Lechterman analyzes accountability as a derivative, procedural value rather than a primary or substantive one. "Accountability's primary job description is to verify compliance with substantive normative principles -- once those principles are settled." The implication: demanding "accountable AI" is insufficient without first specifying the substantive values (justice, privacy, safety) against which accountability is measured. This reframes the question from "whether there should be accountability" to "accountability in light of what."

### Explainability (Ch. 9)

Danks argues that explainable AI (XAI) is an important governance tool but warns that "explanations provided by XAI are not [necessarily] the kind required for governance." There is a fundamental tradeoff between an explanation being **true** and being **intuitive**, and different audiences need different explanations. It is essential to specify governance goals accurately before demanding explainability, otherwise we risk investing in technical solutions that do not serve governance purposes.

### Power (Ch. 10)

Lazar moves beyond a simple analysis ("A has power over B when A can get B to do something B would not otherwise do") to argue that power is perhaps the central problem in AI governance. The question is not just what AI is used for, but **for which ends** power is exercised and **by whom**. Power raises issues of justification, legitimacy, and authority -- three concepts from political philosophy that must be distinguished and applied to AI governance.

### Structural Injustice (Ch. 11)

Lim and Himmelreich argue that the conceptual tool of structural injustice is indispensable for good AI governance. They distinguish different ways AI can be involved in structural injustice and outline a theory that places heavy emphasis on social structural explanations rather than individual harms and benefits. AI can perpetuate, amplify, and create new forms of structural injustice. This approach is preferable to alternative frameworks focused on harms/benefits or mere statements of values.

### Community (Ch. 12)

Viehoff identifies community as a value of high importance in AI governance that is not adequately captured by fairness, explainability, privacy, or justice. AI-driven pathologies of **decommunitarization** include automation of social functions, datafication of human relationships, and the disappearing public sphere. The concept of community is an important analytical lens for understanding both current shortcomings and future risks of AI.

## Key Policy Frameworks

- **Justice-first framework:** Prioritize structural justice over procedural fairness; ensure AI systems do not compound existing injustices even when they appear "fair"
- **Multi-value governance:** No single value (fairness, privacy, accountability, explainability) is sufficient; governance must balance and prioritize among them
- **Power analysis:** Evaluate who holds power in AI systems, how that power is exercised, and whether it is legitimate
- **Substantive accountability:** Define the specific normative principles AI systems must comply with before establishing accountability mechanisms

## Practical Takeaways for University Governance

1. **Audit university AI systems for justice, not just fairness.** An admissions algorithm, grading tool, or student success predictor may appear fair by statistical metrics while compounding structural injustice. Ask: does this system operate on inputs shaped by historical discrimination?

2. **Do not rely on student consent as the primary privacy mechanism.** Following Veliz, consent is insufficient for protecting privacy in contexts of power asymmetry (which characterizes the student-institution relationship). Implement structural privacy protections.

3. **Specify substantive values before demanding accountability.** Before requiring that AI tools used in teaching or administration be "accountable," define the specific principles they must satisfy (equity of access, pedagogical effectiveness, non-discrimination).

4. **Match explainability to audience.** Explanations of AI decisions affecting students (financial aid, academic standing, course recommendations) should be designed for the specific audience, not just technically accurate.

5. **Consider community impact.** AI-driven automation of advising, tutoring, or administrative support may erode the university community. Evaluate whether AI adoption strengthens or weakens the social fabric of the institution.

6. **De-couple decision systems.** Avoid using the same predictive model for multiple high-stakes decisions about students (admissions, financial aid, housing, academic support) to prevent compounding effects.
