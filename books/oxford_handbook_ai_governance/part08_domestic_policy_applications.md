## Comprehensive Summary

This section presents five case studies of AI governance in specific domestic policy domains. Each case illuminates governance challenges that are broadly applicable, including to university settings.

### AI in Government Adjudication -- Social Security (Ch. 38)

Glaze, Ho, Ray, and Tsang present a detailed case study of AI adoption at the U.S. Social Security Administration (SSA) for claims adjudication. Key lessons:
- **Change management is critical:** Successful AI adoption at SSA required individual expertise and persistence, not just top-down mandates
- **Organizational context matters:** What factors led to adoption and which resisted it varied by organizational context and task characteristics
- The case provides broadly applicable lessons about how **administrative agencies can govern the adoption of AI** and the iterative, ground-up process required
- This is described as "a highly relevant, timely, and instructive study" for understanding governmental AI adoption

### Surveillance AI Framework (Ch. 39)

Caines examines surveillance technologies regularly employed by local governments and develops a practical framework for governance. Key concerns:
- **Function creep:** Surveillance systems originally deployed for one purpose expand to serve others
- **Mission creep:** The mission of surveillance programs expands beyond original intent
- The framework helps anticipate major threats and governance challenges on the horizon
- Governance must address the tendency of surveillance to expand rather than remain bounded

### Smart City Technologies (Ch. 40)

Botero Arcila provides a balanced analysis of smart cities through the lens of **political economy**, avoiding both uncritical optimism and blanket opposition:
- Smart cities are recruited by two opposing narratives: utopian opportunity and dystopian risk
- The reality requires attention to both the opportunities of connected urban infrastructure and the risks of computation and surveillance
- The chapter supports deliberation about **institutional interventions** to govern smart city technology
- Analysis must attend to who benefits, who bears costs, and how power is distributed

### AI in Healthcare (Ch. 41)

Aggarwal, Matheny, Shachar, Wang, and Thadaney-Israni contextualize AI within the history of algorithmic use in healthcare and identify governance priorities:
- The **Quintuple Aim** of healthcare (patient outcomes, cost reduction, population impact, provider wellness, equity and inclusion) can guide AI governance in this domain
- **Algorithmic bias risks** are severe: unrepresentative datasets can exacerbate health disparities along racial, gender, and socioeconomic lines
- The authors strongly caution about the need for **diversity, transparency, and accountability** in healthcare AI
- Governance frameworks from healthcare (FDA regulation, clinical trials, informed consent) provide models but need adaptation for AI

### AI in Consumer Finance (Ch. 42)

Aggarwal traces the evolution of financial privacy protections in the age of AI and fintech:
- Consumer finance privacy law has evolved from bank confidentiality to cross-sectoral data protection
- Fintech and AI-driven technologies are the latest force reshaping privacy regulation
- The chapter describes both opportunities (better services, fraud detection) and challenges (data exploitation, discrimination, opacity) of AI in consumer finance
- Governance must evolve to address the unique risks of AI-driven financial decision-making

## Key Policy Frameworks

- **Change management for AI adoption:** Successful institutional AI adoption requires individual champions, organizational persistence, iterative implementation, and attention to organizational culture (SSA case)
- **Function/mission creep framework:** Monitor and constrain the tendency of AI systems to expand beyond their original purpose
- **Political economy analysis:** Evaluate who benefits and who bears costs from AI deployment; attend to power dynamics
- **Quintuple Aim framework (healthcare):** Patient outcomes, cost, population impact, provider wellness, equity -- adaptable to other domains
- **Evolving privacy governance:** Privacy frameworks must evolve as AI creates new data uses and risks

## Practical Takeaways for University Governance

1. **Learn from the SSA case** about institutional AI adoption. Successful adoption at a large public institution required individual champions, organizational learning, iterative deployment, and attention to the specific organizational context. Universities should expect a similar multi-year process of institutional adaptation.

2. **Guard against function and mission creep.** AI systems deployed at universities for one purpose (e.g., learning analytics to improve pedagogy) tend to expand (e.g., to surveillance of student behavior, predictive policing of academic integrity, or performance monitoring of faculty). Establish clear boundaries at deployment and regularly audit for creep.

3. **Apply political economy analysis to campus AI.** When evaluating smart campus technologies, learning management AI, or institutional analytics, ask: who benefits from this system? Who bears its costs? How does it redistribute power among administrators, faculty, students, and commercial vendors?

4. **Adapt the Quintuple Aim** to university AI governance:
   - **Student outcomes:** Does AI improve educational results?
   - **Cost effectiveness:** Does it reduce costs without shifting burdens unfairly?
   - **Institutional impact:** Does it strengthen the institution's mission?
   - **Faculty/staff wellness:** Does it reduce burnout or increase it?
   - **Equity and inclusion:** Does it narrow or widen disparities?

5. **Address privacy comprehensively.** Student educational data, research data, and employee data each raise distinct privacy concerns in the age of AI. Governance must address AI-specific risks (inference, aggregation, profiling) beyond traditional data protection.

6. **Expect algorithmic bias in education AI.** Just as healthcare AI can exacerbate health disparities through unrepresentative data, education AI can exacerbate educational disparities. Audit training data for representativeness and monitor outcomes by demographic group.
