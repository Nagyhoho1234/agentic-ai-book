## Comprehensive Summary

This section examines the nuts and bolts of building a regulatory ecosystem for AI. It moves from descriptive analysis of existing regulatory approaches to prescriptive proposals for new regulatory instruments.

### The EU's "Brussels Effect" (Ch. 13)

Dempsey, McBride, Haataja, and Bryson examine the European Union's approach to transnational AI regulation. The EU's regulatory approach, through instruments such as the AI Act, exerts a "Brussels effect" -- a large global impact on AI regulation worldwide. The chapter explores key EU documents, laws, and approaches to AI regulation, and discusses what the rest of the world might learn from this approach. The EU model emphasizes risk-based classification of AI systems, mandatory conformity assessments for high-risk systems, and transparency requirements.

### A Comprehensive Regulatory Ecosystem (Ch. 14)

Hudson identifies the core components necessary for a complete governance regime of AI decision-making. The chapter explores how basic human rights to know, to appeal, and to litigate interact with AI systems, leading to a governance regime that includes:
- Standard setting
- Training and certification
- Insurance requirements
- Procurement standards
- Identification and registration of AI systems
- Archiving of AI decision records
- Testing and validation

Hudson also addresses the sustainability question: how to render governance both robust and sustainable over time, ensuring it remains "functional, sustainable, and adaptive."

### AI Permit Programs (Ch. 15)

Higgins proposes a specific risk mitigation strategy: a permitting approach whereby government administrators act as market gatekeepers. AI systems would only be allowed to operate commercially if they meet specific technical standards and demonstrate acceptable risk levels, maintaining compliance through individualized permit terms. This approach draws parallels with medical device regulation and the EU's proposed AI framework.

### AI Loyalty by Design (Ch. 16)

Aguirre, Reiner, Surden, and Dempsey address an often-overlooked tension: the conflict of interest between AI creators and end users. In domains like healthcare and law, loyalty to the end user is paramount, yet AI developers' interests may diverge. The authors propose "AI loyalty" as a design principle: AI systems should be designed from the outset to primarily and transparently benefit their end users, or at minimum clearly communicate conflicts of interest when they cannot be eliminated.

### Information Markets for Governance (Ch. 17)

Clark argues that governments need better information to effectively regulate AI. He focuses on using metrics and measures from the AI research community as informal devices of self-regulation, pointing to measures that can be used by governments and their policymaking apparatus to steer AI development by both the private sector and academia. Better information about AI capabilities, risks, and deployments is a prerequisite for effective governance.

### Aligning Regulation to Sociotechnical Change (Ch. 18)

Maas argues that the regulatory ecosystem should move past law-centric and technology-centric silos and instead focus on "how, when, and why AI applications enable patterns of sociotechnical change." He identifies six problem logics for AI regulation:
1. Ethical challenges
2. Security risks
3. Safety risks
4. Structural shifts (in power, economy, society)
5. Public goods (requiring collective action)
6. Governance disruption (AI changing the nature of governance itself)

These problem logics help "improve the regulatory triage, tailoring, timing and responsiveness, and regulatory design of AI policy."

## Key Policy Frameworks

- **Risk-based classification** (EU model): Categorize AI applications by risk level; apply proportionate governance requirements
- **Regulatory ecosystem approach:** Governance is not a single regulation but an interconnected system of standards, permits, insurance, procurement rules, testing, archiving, and appeals processes
- **Loyalty-by-design principle:** Design AI systems with end-user benefit as the primary obligation
- **Six problem logics:** Use ethical, security, safety, structural, public goods, and governance disruption lenses to triage and tailor regulation
- **Information infrastructure:** Build metrics and measurement systems as prerequisites for effective governance

## Practical Takeaways for University Governance

1. **Adopt risk-based classification** for university AI deployments. Categorize AI tools by impact level: low-risk (scheduling, facilities management), medium-risk (course recommendations, library systems), high-risk (admissions scoring, academic integrity detection, student mental health screening). Apply proportionate governance to each level.

2. **Build a university regulatory ecosystem,** not just individual policies. Following Hudson's model, establish interconnected elements: standards for AI procurement, training requirements for staff using AI tools, documentation and archiving of AI-assisted decisions, clear appeals processes for students affected by AI decisions.

3. **Apply the loyalty principle** when evaluating commercial AI tools. Ask: does this tool serve the university and its students, or primarily the vendor's interests? Pay attention to data extraction, lock-in effects, and misaligned optimization targets (e.g., engagement metrics vs. learning outcomes).

4. **Invest in information infrastructure.** Track what AI systems are deployed across the university, what decisions they influence, and what outcomes they produce. Without this basic information, governance is impossible.

5. **Align governance to the type of sociotechnical change** each AI system produces. An AI writing assistant poses different governance challenges (academic integrity, learning outcomes) than an AI surveillance system (privacy, power) or an AI admissions tool (fairness, structural justice).

6. **Watch the "Brussels effect."** EU AI Act requirements will increasingly shape the AI tools available globally. Universities should anticipate these requirements in procurement decisions and institutional policies.
