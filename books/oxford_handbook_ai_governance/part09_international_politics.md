## Comprehensive Summary

This concluding section places AI governance in its global context, examining how geopolitics, international competition, military strategy, regional governance models, and Global South perspectives shape the development and governance of AI.

### US-China AI Competition (Ch. 43)

Ding challenges the dominant "technonationalism" narrative of US-China AI competition (framing it as a zero-sum competitive game between nation states). He proposes an additional lens of **technoglobalism** that highlights:
- Both countries are deeply embedded in transnational networks of research, commerce, and talent
- The technoglobalism frame reveals interdependencies that the competitive frame ignores
- Policy that focuses exclusively on competition risks being limited by not fully considering the pressures and opportunities arising from transnational networks
- Both frames are needed for a complete picture

### Military AI Governance (Ch. 44)

Canfil and Kania examine the current debate around lethal autonomous weapons systems (LAWS) at the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW):
- Their empirical exploration reveals a nuanced picture of which countries engage in these conversations and why
- The disarmament agenda is supported most strongly not by democracies but by developing world nations
- This finding may also reflect the disempowered role of the Global South in AI governance (connecting to Ch. 48)
- Military AI governance discussions are still in early stages with no binding international agreements

### AI and the Balance of Power (Ch. 45)

Horowitz, Pindyck, and Mahoney analyze how AI development may change both relative national power and international interactions:
- Both **military and non-military dimensions** of state power may be influenced by AI
- The stability of the balance of power, international institutional order, and international norms may all be affected
- Key findings: (1) most states are in early stages of AI strategy, (2) states are hedging against first-mover status by countries like the US and China, (3) private sector technological pace complicates state efforts to predict and manage security interests
- AI may change the nature of international competition without radically restructuring international politics

### EU AI Governance (Ch. 46)

Stix traces the EU's path in governing AI, contrasting it with the US and Chinese approaches:
- The EU emphasizes **ethical, trustworthy, and reliable** technological development
- Focus on strengthening the AI ecosystem rather than just regulating risks
- Three strategies for the EU moving forward: (1) AI megaprojects for strategic capability, (2) AI regulatory agencies for enforcement, (3) setting international standards
- The EU model prioritizes human rights, democratic values, and rule of law in AI governance

### AI and International Politics Broadly (Ch. 47)

Arsenault and Kreps examine the general impacts of AI on international affairs:
- International actors may pursue AI for **improving governance efficiency**, capitalizing on military capabilities, expanding power and influence, and global competition
- AI simultaneously creates opportunities for improved coordination and cooperation and risks of invasive surveillance, competition, and military escalation
- The current trends in AI will likely intensify rather than radically restructure international politics
- The technology creates both centripetal (cooperation) and centrifugal (competition) forces

### Global South Perspectives (Ch. 48)

Png provides a critical intervention, systematically documenting the tensions between Global North and Global South in AI governance:
- The dominant narrative of the Global North fails to internalize Global South concerns and challenges
- Three important roles for Global South actors:
  1. **Challenging exclusionary governance mechanisms** that shut out developing world voices
  2. **Providing legitimate expertise** including whole-systems and historical perspectives
  3. **Providing alternative governance mechanisms** such as South-South solidarity, co-governance, democratic accountability, and political economy of resistance
- Three steps for improvement: (1) engage in historical-geopolitical power analysis, (2) identify and mitigate participation barriers, (3) co-construct and formalize Global South roles in governance processes

### NATO and Military AI (Ch. 49)

Stanley-Lockman and Trabucco examine NATO's role in responsible AI governance for military applications:
- NATO can play two particular roles: (1) strategic and policy planning, (2) standards and certification
- These roles should be guided by three AI governance pillars: ethics and values, legal norms, and safety and security
- NATO is "an institution with considerable opportunities to shape responsible AI governance"
- The chapter advocates for establishing foundations for emerging military technology built on informed ethical principles

## Key Policy Frameworks

- **Technonationalism vs. technoglobalism:** Both competitive and cooperative frames are needed to understand AI geopolitics
- **EU rights-based governance model:** Prioritize ethical, trustworthy AI development guided by human rights and democratic values
- **Global South inclusion framework:** Challenge exclusionary governance, legitimize diverse expertise, build alternative mechanisms
- **Military AI governance pillars:** Ethics/values, legal norms, safety/security
- **Balance of power analysis:** Evaluate how AI affects military power, economic power, institutional order, and international norms

## Practical Takeaways for University Governance

1. **Understand the geopolitical context** of AI tools adopted at the university. Supply chains, data sovereignty, and regulatory requirements differ between US, EU, and Chinese AI ecosystems. Universities operating internationally must navigate these differences.

2. **Align with EU standards proactively.** The EU AI Act is becoming a global benchmark. Universities that adopt EU-aligned governance practices (risk classification, transparency requirements, fundamental rights impact assessments) will be well-positioned regardless of future regulatory developments.

3. **Include diverse perspectives** in AI governance. Following Png, actively seek voices that are typically excluded from AI governance discussions -- including students from underrepresented backgrounds, staff in lower-status positions, and international perspectives. Avoid governance structures that replicate Global North dominance patterns.

4. **Address dual-use research concerns.** Universities produce AI research that may have military or surveillance applications. Following the military AI governance discussions, establish clear policies on dual-use AI research, engagement with military funding, and responsible disclosure.

5. **Prepare for a fragmented regulatory landscape.** Different jurisdictions are developing different AI governance requirements. Universities with international operations (branch campuses, research collaborations, student recruitment) must build governance flexible enough to comply with multiple frameworks.

6. **Engage with international AI governance standards-setting.** Universities can play a unique role as relatively trusted, non-commercial actors in international AI governance discussions. Following the NATO model, universities can contribute to standards, certification, and ethical frameworks.
