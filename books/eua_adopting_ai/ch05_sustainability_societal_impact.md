# 5. Sustainability and Societal Impact

**Pages:** 23--27 | **Source:** EUA Task-and-Finish Group on AI (January 2026)

## Comprehensive Summary

This concluding substantive chapter broadens the lens to consider the consequences of AI adoption that stretch beyond the immediate university context -- environmental sustainability, social justice, wellbeing, inclusivity, and the fundamental question of what constitutes reasonable and responsible use. It argues that universities, as integral parts of the social fabric, have a responsibility to shape broader attitudes towards AI in society.

### Environmental Impacts

Using AI in universities has consequences that extend far beyond institutional boundaries. The report raises three hard questions:

1. How can universities reconcile AI's climate impact with carbon neutrality ambitions?
2. How can AI technologies preserve and enhance diversity and wellbeing within the institutional community?
3. How can universities' actions encourage a deeper understanding of technologies and a healthy attitude towards AI in society?

**Key environmental concerns:**
- The cumulative impact of one-off AI uses on resource consumption and ultimately climate change is the immediate concern
- Energy consumption varies greatly depending on specific use: training a foundation model for a discipline-related purpose generates a large immediate impact; individual learner queries use fewer resources but aggregate impact across all learners may be much larger
- Projections for future consumption are imprecise and depend on both uptake rates and efficiency improvements
- Users are often **geographically and psychologically removed** from the environmental consequences -- alienated from the impacts of large-scale data centres (water use, mineral mining, e-waste, energy demand)
- The shift in power demand represents not just a linear increase but a magnitude change that challenges current sustainable energy ambitions
- Providers and governments are considering commissioning new nuclear power plants despite unresolved long-term waste issues
- Few users think about rare earths mined in Inner Mongolia or Tanzania when writing a prompt to ChatGPT

Universities have a key role in building awareness of the vast resources (human and energy) that AI consumes. This will become essential as energy consumption increases, even among casual users.

### Social Impacts

The wider social justice dimension comes into sharper focus when confronting uncomfortable realities of AI use:

- **Exploitative labour practices:** Human trainers in large generative AI models tend to be workers in the Global South (Kenya, Philippines, Venezuela), systematically exposed to highly disturbing content, often at damage to their mental health, for low wages
- **Structural inequality:** Value accrues to AI firms in the Global North while health risks and low wages are borne by labour in the Global South
- **Global South vulnerability:** Emerging countries have tended to adopt AI quickly with comparatively little concern for protecting privacy and retaining control of their data
- **Global North privilege:** Developed countries have the luxury of taking a long-term view, where slower, steadier uptake ensures privacy and data sovereignty

However, AI also brings benefits to disadvantaged countries: skilled employment, increased access to free informational and educational resources, applications in agriculture, healthcare, and climate resilience. Universities have a duty to encourage such initiatives while mitigating social harms.

### Impact on the Institution -- Safeguarding Wellbeing Among Users

The pressure from private providers promising efficiency gains, combined with fear of being left behind in the AI race, can prompt university leaders to make hasty decisions without sufficient thought about whether AI actually improves work or learning experiences.

**Key wellbeing concerns:**
- Identifying where AI adds real value versus where it is potentially damaging requires competencies in psychology, human-machine interaction, pedagogy, and cognition
- Overreliance on AI may diminish students' autonomy and sense of agency, particularly those who assume certain rationality is embedded in AI-generated output
- Students may come to depend on AI for inspiration, tutoring, or brainstorming, overlooking the importance of human-to-human interaction
- Concerns about AI dependence feed into wider debates around digital culture, digital sobriety, and wellbeing
- There is a widely held concern among university professionals that learners' mental health has been in decline since Covid-19; mainstreaming AI without adequate training on risks and limitations may compound a worsening mental health crisis

**Recommended mitigation:** Universities should implement structures for systematic dialogue and exchange among users, and establish where vulnerabilities exist. Safeguarding human relationships and prioritising wellbeing in the institutional setting is essential.

### Widening Participation in Higher Education -- Can AI Be the Great "Leveller"?

The report presents both sides of the debate:

**Risks:** Some argue AI will amplify differences or inequalities in student populations. Growing evidence that AI may exacerbate the digital divide makes it all the more important to design **inclusive AI solutions** that intentionally account for differences such as neurodivergent learning styles, linguistic backgrounds, and cultural contexts.

**Opportunities:** Systems based on AI can be beneficial for engaging neurodiverse learners who struggle in traditional classroom environments. AI can offer personalised pathways adapted to individual styles, needs, or ambitions. Technologies can create online content (avatars, chatbots) available to lifelong learners who prioritise flexible timing.

Close monitoring of these solutions is essential to detect and address any hidden biases or discriminatory effects.

### Outsourcing of Competence -- What Constitutes Reasonable and Responsible Use?

This section poses the report's most fundamental philosophical question. As generative AI developed rapidly, governments and industry scrambled to be seen as "first movers." Universities are trying to balance this pressure with a healthy degree of caution, tending towards cultures that promote reasonable and responsible use while also recognising the need to experiment.

**Key tensions:**
- At the institutional level, there may be a convenience in using AI to produce essays more quickly or as part of planning and executing scientific experiments -- but if this repeatedly compromises fundamental academic skills or research principles, universities need to rethink their approach
- AI should not be used only to make work easier; instead, its potential to enhance learning and deepen knowledge should be central
- At a societal level, AI raises questions about individual and collective capacity for critical thinking and whether this will be diminished if we externalise what we consider "human" competence to machines
- Efficiency should not be a pedagogical goal -- not everything (curiosity, for example) can or should be measured in terms of efficiency
- When much of our work is automated using non-transparent AI models, questions arise about which processes require human intervention and which can reasonably be performed using AI, without compromising reasoning, judgement, or integrity

### Conclusion

The report concludes that universities, as integral parts of the social fabric, will shape attitudes towards new technologies in the workplace, civil society, and cultural and domestic spheres. Technologies have advanced quickly, with the potential to transform how universities work. But positive, meaningful change takes time. Rather than blindly pursuing efficiency and productivity, universities can -- and should -- take a more deliberate and reflective approach to AI adoption, rooted in human values and guided by their core missions.

This begins with building awareness of possibilities and risks, cultivating shared ownership of the process, and creating space for reflection on the real and lasting value of digital tools. Universities must take a balanced, human-centred approach that upholds ethical principles, protects academic values, and safeguards the wellbeing of academic and student communities.

## Key Recommendations

1. **Build environmental awareness** -- educate the university community about the vast resources (energy, water, minerals, human labour) that AI consumes
2. **Confront social justice implications** -- acknowledge and address the exploitative labour practices and structural inequalities embedded in AI supply chains
3. **Safeguard wellbeing** -- implement structures for systematic dialogue, establish where vulnerabilities exist, and prioritise human relationships in the institutional setting
4. **Design inclusive AI solutions** -- intentionally account for neurodivergent learning styles, linguistic backgrounds, and cultural contexts to avoid exacerbating the digital divide
5. **Monitor AI solutions closely** for hidden biases or discriminatory effects, especially those designed to widen participation
6. **Resist the "first mover" pressure** -- balance the urge to adopt quickly with a healthy degree of caution and a commitment to reasonable, responsible use
7. **Steer conversation away from pure efficiency** -- AI should not be used merely to make work easier; its potential to enhance learning and deepen knowledge should be central
8. **Preserve "human" competences** -- maintain clarity about which processes require human intervention and which can reasonably be automated without compromising reasoning, judgement, or integrity
9. **Take a long-term view** -- meaningful change takes time; prioritise deliberate and reflective adoption over speed

## Practical Takeaways for University Leaders

- **Commission an environmental audit** of your institution's AI use: what tools are being used, how often, and what is the estimated energy and resource footprint?
- **Include sustainability criteria** in AI procurement decisions -- ask vendors about energy consumption, data centre locations, and environmental policies
- **Create wellbeing support structures** specifically for AI-related concerns: student dependency, mental health impacts, erosion of human interaction
- **Design for inclusivity from the start** -- do not treat accessibility as an afterthought; involve diverse user groups in the design and testing of AI-based tools
- **Host difficult conversations** about what should remain "human" in academic work -- where do you draw the line between augmentation and outsourcing of competence?
- **Communicate your values externally** -- universities shape societal attitudes; be explicit about your ethical approach to AI and engage with government and civil society on these questions
- **Do not conflate convenience with value** -- just because AI can automate a task does not mean it should; evaluate each use case against mission alignment and quality of outcomes
