# 4. Regulation and Compliance

**Source:** EUA (2026), *Adopting AI that serves the needs and values of universities*, pp. 20--22.

---

## Comprehensive Summary

This chapter examines the most significant upcoming regulatory challenge for European universities: implementing the EU AI Act (2024). It covers risk classification in education, the role of national surveillance authorities, the opportunity for early dialogue, the benefits of pooling expertise across institutions, and practical challenges at the institutional level.

### Risk Levels, Controls and Compliance (p. 20)

The EU's approach to AI has been orientated towards trust: only when users trust AI will they engage with it and reap the benefits. The AI Act regulates technology according to the level of risk that particular uses carry. In education, the use of AI for access, evaluation and monitoring of learners is classified as high risk. Therefore, related practices must adhere to high standards in terms of training data and human oversight. Where risk is considered unacceptable -- for example, in emotion tracking -- the use of AI is forbidden. While risk levels are clearly delineated, there are still grey zones for universities in terms of which practices are AI Act-compliant and which are not. For example, AI systems for assessing the appropriate level of education an individual will receive or will be able to access carry high risk. This could apply to adaptive learning using AI, meaning related activities would have to comply with requirements for training data and include provision for human oversight. AI systems used for evaluating learning outcomes could similarly be subject to high-risk requirements. The stricter rules are expected to apply in 2026, and in the meantime universities are working towards the cause-no-detriment principle in their experimentation.

### Overseeing Compliance at the National Level (p. 21)

The AI Act foresees a system wherein national bodies are mandated to implement and monitor compliance with the new regulation. As it stands, many of these national bodies are working to get a better understanding of the actual implications for various sectors. For some, their regulatory power has traditionally been limited to product safety and market surveillance. Overseeing compliance in higher education represents a new competence for many of these bodies, and they will have to learn fast to roll out implementation nationally while ensuring alignment with European standards and expectations. It is important that this be a coordinated effort to avoid repeating the mistakes of GDPR adoption, where, for instance, the same proctoring tools were considered acceptable by some agencies while not by others.

### An Opportunity for Dialogue (p. 21)

The newness of the approach necessitates early-stage dialogue between universities and authorities. By opening dialogue channels early, universities can put forward their case for AI systems in education that are safe and trustworthy, but not so restrictive as to block the advantages that AI can offer learners and university staff alike. Cultivating a positive relationship with national surveillance authorities is especially important given that the AI Act is just one of many regulatory challenges universities face. The recent wave of European digital regulation -- on cybersecurity, research security and more -- presents similar areas where engagement is needed. Member state governments are tasked with designating authorities to deal with the AI Act at the national level, often by expanding the scope of established agencies. These agencies will also act as a bridge to the European AI office, tasked with coordinating implementation among member states.

### Pooling Expertise for the Benefit of All (p. 22)

It would be advantageous for universities to engage with these agencies via the bodies that represent them at national level (i.e., university associations or rectors' conferences). This would amplify universities' common concerns and priorities, and could afford individual institutions the benefits of pooling their academic, legal and technical expertise. Such pooling of resources could be useful for monitoring compliance requirements, understanding their implications, and laying the groundwork for optimising and harmonising universities' use of AI within the regional or national context.

### Challenges at the Institutional Level (p. 22)

While dialogue at the national level will ease the transition, challenges will present themselves at the individual institution. Education technology providers will develop AI-based systems for assessment and other purposes. Units responsible for purchasing such products will need to be well equipped to judge whether the product is AI Act-compliant. The relevant units or user communities will also need to be adequately resourced to meet the compliance obligations required for deployers of high-risk systems, including human oversight, keeping logs and having the overall technical capacity to use the system. Besides managing formal institutional-level partnerships with technology providers, the informal use of AI as "shadow IT" will still carry risks. Many teachers have already built personal routines for using AI in examinations using readily available models like ChatGPT or Copilot -- models that by no means meet European standards for high-risk uses. The transition from free experimentation to adherence to institutional rules will need to be carefully managed, requiring active participation from all parts of the university's community and adequate training of users.

## Key Recommendations

1. **Understand the AI Act's risk classification** -- education uses involving access, evaluation and monitoring of learners are classified as high risk
2. **Prepare for the stricter rules expected in 2026** -- adopt the "cause-no-detriment" principle during the transition period
3. **Open dialogue with national surveillance authorities early** -- make the case for AI in education that is safe but not overly restrictive
4. **Engage through national university associations** to pool legal, technical and academic expertise and amplify common concerns
5. **Avoid repeating GDPR mistakes** -- push for coordinated, harmonised implementation across member states
6. **Equip procurement units** to assess whether AI products are compliant with the AI Act before purchasing
7. **Address shadow IT risks** -- manage the transition from informal personal AI use to compliant institutional practices
8. **Resource compliance properly** -- ensure units deploying high-risk AI systems have the capacity for human oversight, logging and technical management

---

## Practical Takeaways for University Leaders

- Map all current AI uses across the institution against the AI Act's risk categories (unacceptable, high, limited, minimal risk)
- Invest in legal and compliance expertise on the AI Act now, before the stricter provisions take effect in 2026
- Engage proactively with your national AI surveillance authority -- do not wait to be regulated; shape the conversation
- Work through your national rectors' conference or university association to pool expertise and present a unified sector voice
- Develop clear procurement guidelines requiring AI Act compliance assessment for all new educational technology purchases
- Address shadow IT head-on: acknowledge that staff are already using commercial AI tools, and provide compliant alternatives
- Provide training to all staff involved in deploying or purchasing AI systems on compliance obligations, including human oversight and record-keeping requirements
- Build institutional capacity (personnel, technical infrastructure, governance processes) to meet high-risk system deployer obligations
