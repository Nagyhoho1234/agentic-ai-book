# 4. Regulation and Compliance

**Pages:** 20--22 | **Source:** EUA Task-and-Finish Group on AI (January 2026)

## Comprehensive Summary

This chapter focuses on the most significant upcoming regulatory challenge for universities: the implementation of the European Union's AI Act (2024). It examines how the risk-based regulatory framework applies to higher education, the challenges of compliance at national and institutional levels, and the opportunities for universities to shape the dialogue.

### Risk Levels, Controls and Compliance

The EU's AI Act takes a trust-based, risk-oriented approach. The premise is that users will only trust and engage with AI when they believe the systems are trustworthy. The Act regulates technology according to the level of risk that particular uses carry.

**Key implications for higher education:**
- The use of AI for **access, evaluation, and monitoring of learners** is considered **high risk** under the AI Act, requiring high standards in terms of training data and human oversight
- Where risk is considered **unacceptable** -- such as **emotion tracking** -- the use of AI is **forbidden**
- AI systems used to assess the appropriate level of education an individual will receive or access carry high risk, meaning adaptive learning using AI could trigger compliance requirements
- AI systems used for evaluating learning outcomes could also be subject to high-risk requirements
- The stricter rules are expected to apply in 2026

**Grey zones remain:** There are still unclear areas regarding which university practices are AI Act-compliant and which are not. Universities are becoming accustomed to a certain level of ambiguity and working towards the cause-no-detriment principle in their experimentation.

### Overseeing Compliance at the National Level

The AI Act foresees a system wherein national bodies are mandated to implement and monitor compliance with the new regulation. Many national bodies are still working to understand the actual implications for various sectors, and for some, their regulatory power has traditionally been limited to product safety and market surveillance.

Overseeing AI compliance in higher education represents a **new competence** for many national bodies. They will need to learn fast to roll out implementation nationally while ensuring alignment with European standards. The report warns against repeating mistakes made during GDPR adoption, where the same tools were considered acceptable by some agencies but not others.

Member state governments are designating authorities to deal with the AI Act at the national level, which will also act as a bridge to the European AI Office coordinating implementation among member states.

### An Opportunity for Dialogue

The newness of the regulatory approach creates an opportunity for early-stage dialogue between universities and authorities. By opening dialogue channels early, universities can put forward their case for AI systems in education that are safe and trustworthy but not so restrictive as to block the advantages AI can offer learners and staff.

Cultivating a positive relationship with national surveillance authorities would serve the higher education sector well, especially given that the AI Act is just one of many regulatory challenges universities face (cybersecurity regulation, research security, etc.).

### Pooling Expertise for the Benefit of All

Universities should engage with national regulatory agencies via representative bodies (university associations, rectors' conferences). This would:
- Amplify universities' common concerns and priorities
- Afford individual institutions the benefits of pooling academic, legal, and technical expertise
- Be useful for monitoring compliance requirements, understanding implications, and laying groundwork for optimising AI use within the regional or national context

### Challenges at the Institutional Level

At the institutional level, several challenges emerge:
- **Procurement competence:** Units responsible for buying AI-based systems (e.g., for assessment) must be well equipped to judge AI Act compliance
- **Resource requirements:** Relevant units or user communities will need to be adequately resourced to meet compliance obligations for deployers of high-risk systems (human oversight, log-keeping, technical capacity)
- **"Shadow IT" risks:** Informal use of AI (teachers using ChatGPT or Copilot personally for examinations) will continue regardless of institutional policies, and these readily-available models do not necessarily meet European standards for high-risk uses
- **Transition management:** Moving from free experimentation to adherence to institutional rules must be carefully managed, requiring active participation from all parts of the university community and adequate training
- **AI governance importance:** The great importance of AI governance is ensuring universities get the best possible benefits of AI while keeping risks under control

## Key Recommendations

1. Prepare now for the **stricter AI Act rules expected in 2026**, particularly around high-risk uses in education (assessment, evaluation, access decisions)
2. **Open early dialogue** with national regulatory authorities to shape how the AI Act applies to higher education
3. **Pool expertise** through university associations and rectors' conferences to monitor compliance requirements and share resources
4. **Build procurement competence** -- ensure units purchasing AI-based systems can assess AI Act compliance
5. **Resource compliance properly** -- units deploying high-risk AI systems need capacity for human oversight, log-keeping, and technical management
6. **Address "shadow IT"** -- recognise that informal AI use will persist and develop strategies to manage it rather than ignore it
7. **Manage the transition carefully** from free experimentation to regulated use, with active participation and adequate training
8. Avoid repeating **GDPR adoption mistakes** by pursuing coordinated, consistent interpretation of regulations across institutions and countries

## Practical Takeaways for University Leaders

- **Map your high-risk AI uses now:** Identify all AI systems used for student assessment, admission, monitoring, or adaptive learning -- these are likely high-risk under the AI Act
- **Designate responsibility:** Determine who within the institution is responsible for AI Act compliance and ensure they have adequate resources and expertise
- **Engage nationally:** Join or establish dialogue with national AI regulatory bodies through your university association; do not wait for regulations to be imposed
- **Audit vendor compliance:** Develop clear policies and checklists for assessing whether vendor-provided AI tools meet AI Act requirements
- **Acknowledge and manage shadow IT:** Many staff are already using personal AI tools for professional tasks; develop pragmatic policies rather than pretending this does not happen
- **Plan the transition:** Create a clear communication and training plan for moving from exploratory AI use to regulated, compliant use
- **Watch for 2026 deadlines:** The stricter rules will take effect in 2026; institutions should aim to be substantially prepared well before this date
