# 2. Institutional Strategies

**Source:** EUA (2026), *Adopting AI that serves the needs and values of universities*, pp. 10--14.

---

## Comprehensive Summary

This chapter examines how universities can develop and implement institutional strategies for AI adoption that respect their missions, allow for continued experimentation, connect with national and European priorities, start small to manage risk, and balance ambition with momentum.

### AI Strategies That Respect Institutional Missions (p. 10)

Following the initial "learn-by-doing" phase after the launch of ChatGPT in 2022, universities now face the challenge of converting exploratory experience into long-term strategy and policy. The report argues that AI adoption should first and foremost uphold university values and serve institutional missions. Rather than uncritically following AI and all that it promises, universities should set out clear, sustainable and ethical ambitions for the evolution of AI in higher education and research. In the few short years since generative AI became widely accessible, universities have become increasingly aware of the need to keep their missions intact. Many institutions initially articulated guiding principles on AI use through stand-alone strategy documents. The focus has since shifted towards embedding AI in institutions' wider strategies, and institutional strategies should continue to enable experimentation even as they consolidate.

### Strategies That Allow for Experimentation and Prioritisation (pp. 10--11)

While preserving agreed strategic goals is important, this should not prevent universities from considering their approach to AI. Many institutions have articulated guiding principles through stand-alone documents, but the focus has shifted towards embedding AI in wider institutional strategies. Universities will need to prioritise -- identifying "intelligent" pilots and backing initiatives that can be scaled up to provide useful services aligned with each institution's strategy and mission. Institutions with bigger capacity will find this easier; access to funding could well be the differentiator. Universities with smaller budgets will not have the same resources to develop, access or experiment with tools as their better-financed counterparts. The same applies to institutions with restricted strategic capacity. The Jisc AI Maturity model for Education is cited as a useful framework, presenting five stages: Approaching and understanding; Experimenting and exploring; Operational; Embedded; and Optimised/Transformed.

### Linking Strategies with National Priorities and European Initiatives (p. 12)

The discussion of AI in learning and teaching has been lively, but strategies for research and operations have been relatively overshadowed. A more concerning trend is the absence of any real connection between institutional responses and national-level initiatives. European coordination frameworks such as the European Digital Education Hub and AI4Education, as well as collaborative consortia for shared infrastructure, can provide valuable reference points. Cooperation agreements can be useful for sharing experiences, negotiating with vendors and strengthening institutions' visibility. As institutional strategies mature, they will likely encompass AI adoption in all performance dimensions: education, research, knowledge transfer and institutional management. Examples of national AI strategies include Scotland's national advisory group on AI, Spain's national AI strategy (approved May 2024), Finland's AuroraAI programme and France's AI for Humanity strategy.

### Starting Small -- Strategies That Enable Incremental Adoption (p. 13)

Robust strategies are essential for setting and advancing institutional priorities, but strategising for AI implementation across all university functions presents a new challenge. The report strongly advocates a phased, "micro-ambitious" approach with clear steps to de-risk embedding AI into already complex institutional ecosystems. Piloting initiatives without over-committing resources yields multiple benefits: it allows communities to identify use cases that provide institutional value, helps identify tasks to automate, and reveals hidden costs of AI. A slower, more measured approach also gives institutions time to understand their data and identify what data they need to mainstream AI. University representatives participating in EUA workshops showed a clear appetite for investment in AI literacy and technical upskilling at the individual level as a means of equipping institutions for larger-scale projects. The introduction of AI was also seen as an opportunity to refocus attention on developing core academic skills, such as critical thinking.

### Balancing Strategies as Momentum Quickens (p. 14)

University leaders are increasingly recognising that data occupies a new place in strategic decision making. Institutional strategies need to make provisions for building a healthy data culture. Formulating clear policies for assessing vendor-provided tools and educating communities on regulatory dimensions can help prevent non-compliant initiatives from being scaled up. Without the culture and capacity to support implementation, strategies can fall short of expectations, causing disillusionment and deterring exploration. Universities should seek to accommodate a discovery process. By developing strategies that encourage and reward possibilities for exploration (rather than limit them), universities can build digital literacy, drive technological advancement and shed light on the value of interdisciplinarity in optimising AI. Strategies will have to serve the institutional community first but also align with Europe's broader goals for open data and digital sovereignty.

## Key Recommendations

1. **Embed AI in broader institutional strategies** rather than treating it as a stand-alone concern
2. **Maintain space for experimentation** even as strategies become more consolidated and mature
3. **Prioritise intelligent pilots** -- identify initiatives that can be scaled and that align with the institution's mission
4. **Use maturity models** (e.g., Jisc AI Maturity model) to assess progress and plan next steps
5. **Connect institutional strategies to national and European frameworks** (European Digital Education Hub, AI4Education, national AI strategies)
6. **Start small with a "micro-ambitious" approach** -- clear, phased steps that de-risk AI integration
7. **Build a healthy data culture** -- invest in data governance and management processes alongside AI strategy
8. **Foster cooperation agreements** for sharing experiences, pooling resources and negotiating with technology vendors
9. **Balance ambition with capacity** -- avoid strategies that assume AI-driven productivity gains before they are proven

---

## Practical Takeaways for University Leaders

- Audit your institution's current position against the Jisc AI Maturity model (five stages from "Approaching and understanding" to "Optimised/Transformed")
- Do not over-commit resources to unproven AI initiatives; pilot first, evaluate impact outcomes (not just adoption metrics), then scale
- Ensure AI strategy addresses all institutional functions -- education, research, operations and knowledge transfer -- not just learning and teaching
- Engage with national AI advisory bodies and European coordination frameworks to avoid reinventing the wheel
- Invest in data governance infrastructure now; successful AI adoption depends on data maturity
- Create incentives for bottom-up experimentation while maintaining top-down strategic coherence
- Consider cooperative procurement and shared-infrastructure models to reduce costs, especially for smaller institutions
