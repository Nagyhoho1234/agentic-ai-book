# 2. Institutional Strategies

**Pages:** 10--14 | **Source:** EUA Task-and-Finish Group on AI (January 2026)

## Comprehensive Summary

This chapter addresses how universities should develop strategic approaches to AI adoption, emphasising that the sector is transitioning from an exploratory phase to longer-term, systematic integration. The key challenge is converting experimentation into coherent institutional strategy and policy.

### From Experimentation to Strategy

Following the advent of widespread generative AI in 2022, most institutions took a learn-by-doing approach. A pilot phase provided time to establish how best to use opportunities and minimise risks. Now, higher education institutions face the challenge of converting experimentation into long-term strategy and policy. Sharing and consolidating experiences across the sector will be key.

### AI Strategies that Respect Institutional Missions

Universities have long used strategies to guide priorities and communicate values. The recent AI hype pressured institutions to react immediately but also think strategically. Most have come to understand the importance of aligning AI adoption with core missions and community interests. Rather than uncritically following AI and all that it promises, universities should set out clear, sustainable, and ethical ambitions for AI in higher education and research.

The practical application of AI should, first and foremost, uphold university values and serve institutional missions. Stand-alone AI strategy documents with broad guiding principles continue to hold value, but the focus has shifted towards embedding AI in institutions' wider strategies.

### Strategies that Allow for Experimentation and Prioritisation

While preserving agreed strategic goals, institutions should not let this prevent them from considering their approach to AI. Many have articulated guiding principles through stand-alone strategy documents, but the focus now shifts to embedding AI within wider institutional strategies while still enabling experimentation.

Universities will need to prioritise -- identifying "intelligent" pilots that can be scaled up to provide useful services aligned with each institution's strategy and mission. For institutions with bigger capacity, a see-what-works approach will be easier; for others, access to funding could be the differentiator.

### Using Maturity Models

The report highlights the **Jisc AI Maturity Model for Education** as a useful framework for measuring progress, with five stages:
1. **Approaching and understanding** -- interest in AI, understanding its impact
2. **Experimenting and exploring** -- initial guidance, pilots within existing processes
3. **Operational** -- institutional AI principles established, systematic approach to AI skills and literacy
4. **Embedded** -- AI embedded in strategy, mature processes for lifecycle management
5. **Optimised/Transformed** -- AI supports delivery of learning, right tasks automated, staff time freed for creativity and human interaction

### Linking Strategies with National Priorities and European Initiatives

Widespread access to generative AI tools has created lively debate among learning and teaching communities, but strategies for research and operations have been relatively overshadowed. A more concerning trend is the absence of real connection between institutional responses and national-level initiatives.

European coordination frameworks such as the **European Digital Education Hub** and **AI4Education** can provide valuable reference points. Similarly, collaborative consortia and shared-infrastructure models may help smaller institutions overcome resource disparities. Examples of national engagement include:
- **Scotland:** National advisory group on AI
- **Spain:** National AI strategy approved May 2024
- **Finland:** AuroraAI programme
- **France:** AI for Humanity strategy

Universities have been largely absent from conversations about AI adoption in wider society and compliance with European regulation. As conversations advance, universities must develop more outward-looking strategies.

### Starting Small -- Strategies that Enable Incremental Adoption

Robust strategies are essential but strategising for AI implementation across all university functions presents a new challenge. Key insights:

- It would be premature to develop strategies assuming AI-driven increases in productivity or trying to quantify workforce capacity gains
- A phased, "micro-ambitious" approach with clear steps will de-risk embedding AI into the institutional ecosystem
- Piloting initiatives without over-committing resources yields multiple benefits: identifying use cases with real institutional value, identifying tasks to automate, and revealing hidden costs of AI
- Universities have been slow to mature their data management processes, but the realisation that successful AI adoption depends on robust data governance has propelled the conversation forward

### Balancing Strategies as Momentum Quickens

Without culture and capacity to support implementation, strategies can fall short and cause disillusionment. In the case of AI, this could deter exploration among institutional communities. Sustaining confidence depends on balancing ambition with capacity and ethical assurance, and highlighting benefits of AI use for individuals and institutions.

Strategies should serve the institutional community first but also align with Europe's broader goals for open data and digital sovereignty. Institutions must weigh the likely benefits versus the cost of investing in pilot initiatives, and most importantly, generate a risk-appetite without compromising ethical standards or the community's wellbeing.

## Key Recommendations

1. Align AI adoption with **institutional missions** rather than following hype or pressure to move fast
2. Embed AI within wider institutional strategies rather than treating it as a stand-alone topic
3. Use **maturity models** (such as the Jisc framework) to assess current position and plan progression
4. **Prioritise and pilot** -- identify scalable initiatives rather than trying to do everything at once
5. Connect institutional strategies with **national and European initiatives** (European Digital Education Hub, AI4Education, national AI strategies)
6. Invest in **data governance and data maturity** as a prerequisite for successful AI adoption
7. Develop strategies that **encourage and reward exploration** rather than limit possibilities
8. Formulate clear policies for **assessing vendor-provided tools** and educating communities on regulatory dimensions
9. Build cooperation agreements for sharing experiences, negotiating with vendors, and strengthening institutional visibility

## Practical Takeaways for University Leaders

- **Resist over-investment in speculative AI productivity gains:** Do not build strategies around assumed efficiency increases that have not been demonstrated
- **Start with pilots:** Identify 2--3 concrete, scalable use cases aligned with your mission; pilot them before committing institution-wide resources
- **Assess your AI maturity level** using frameworks like the Jisc model to understand where you stand and set realistic next steps
- **Connect outward:** Engage with national AI advisory bodies and European frameworks; universities have been too absent from these conversations
- **Invest in data governance now:** Successful AI adoption depends on robust data management, and most universities are behind on this
- **Create a data culture:** University leaders should recognise that data has come to occupy a new place in strategic decision-making
- **Balance ambition with support:** Ensure the institutional community feels supported, not overwhelmed, by AI adoption initiatives
