# The Future of K-12 Assessments

## Part VIII: AI, Assessments, and Admissions

## Comprehensive Summary

Part VIII tackles assessment and admissions. Khan begins by acknowledging that standardized tests are deeply unpopular but argues against abandoning them entirely. His position: the solution is not to eliminate measurement but to make it richer, more frequent, more transparent, and more actionable.

Khan identifies common critiques of standardized tests: they are too narrow (primarily multiple-choice), take away from learning time, produce results too late to be actionable, and can be biased against certain demographic groups. He addresses each:

- **Narrowness:** AI can make assessments richer. Instead of multiple-choice, AI can engage students in conversations, have them explain reasoning, design experiments, or work through simulations. The assessment begins to resemble a conversation with a thoughtful mentor rather than a bubble-sheet exam.
- **Timeliness:** AI enables continuous assessment embedded in daily practice. Every interaction on Khan Academy generates data about what students know and can do. This replaces the "once or twice a year" snapshot with an ongoing, high-resolution picture.
- **Transparency:** Adaptive assessments drawing from large question banks can be made available to stakeholders without compromising test security, since every student receives a unique sequence of questions.
- **Bias:** Khan acknowledges AI can introduce bias but argues it can also be audited for bias more systematically than human assessors. He notes that current human-driven assessments (like PhD thesis defenses and job interviews) are inconsistent and rife with bias.

Khan describes how AI can assess skills traditionally considered immeasurable: communication, creativity, collaboration, and critical thinking. In science, the AI might assess how well a student can design an experiment or critique a research paper. In reading, it might have a fluid conversation about an author's intent rather than asking four-choice questions.

Khan presents data from Khan Academy's MAP Accelerator program: students who used standardized test scores to inform personalized practice and engaged for 30+ minutes per week exceeded growth projections by 26-38%.

## Key Definitions

- **Summative Assessment:** End-of-period tests designed to measure overall student performance (e.g., state standardized tests, SATs).
- **Continuous Assessment:** Ongoing measurement embedded in daily learning activities, providing real-time data on student progress.
- **Adaptive Assessment:** Tests that adjust question difficulty and content based on student responses, creating a unique experience for each student.
- **MAP Accelerator:** Khan Academy's program using standardized test data to create personalized practice pathways for students.

## Practical Takeaways for Scientists

- Continuous assessment embedded in daily work is more informative than periodic evaluations. Consider implementing ongoing skill tracking for trainees rather than relying solely on annual reviews.
- AI-driven adaptive assessment could transform qualifying exams, comprehensive exams, and other gatekeeping assessments in academia.
- The ability to assess "immeasurable" skills (experimental design, critical analysis) through AI conversation has direct implications for evaluating research competence.
- Bias auditing of AI assessment systems should become standard practice, just as peer review processes are periodically evaluated for bias.
