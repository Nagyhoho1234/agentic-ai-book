# Ethics and Sustainability (Pillar 4)

## Summary

This pillar evaluates whether the AI solution creates a net positive impact from an ethical and sustainability perspective. A responsible AI system upholds ethical standards of fairness, transparency, inclusivity, and accountability while being environmentally sustainable and balancing profit with social responsibility.

### Key themes:

- **Internal accountability is essential** -- Dedicated stakeholders must take responsibility for various facets of the AI implementation process (e.g., chief analytics officer or chief data officer overseeing data governance, dedicated teams for change management, execution tracking)
- **Eliminating bias** should be one of the top priorities during selection and deployment. Bias can enter through the training data set or during training interaction with end users. The solution provider must have sufficient checks and balances
- **Ethical mindset** -- The supplier's development team must be aware and consciously adopt ethical standards while designing and building the system
- **Diversity and inclusivity** -- Prioritizing diverse and inclusive teams from the initial stages can pre-empt biased outcomes
- **Sustainability metrics** are just as important as performance metrics. AI in its current form is not a sustainable solution. Sustainability means developing AI systems compatible with sustaining environmental resources for present and future generations
- **Companies should have a vetting process** as part of their AI system's pre-implementation review to ensure ethical and sustainable considerations have been addressed

### Seven Core Values for Ethical AI (Table 6)

1. **Common good** -- AI solutions should accelerate development of society and improve quality of life
2. **Transparency** -- Decision-making should be easily explainable; developers should be transparent about purpose and intentions
3. **Equitability** -- Designers should keep equitable outcomes for all, from vulnerable communities to people with disabilities
4. **Accountability** -- All stakeholders should be compliant with standards and accountable for outcomes
5. **Inclusiveness** -- Diverse and inclusive design teams help proactively address biases
6. **Non-maleficence** -- AI should not put humans at risk of harm, intended or unintended
7. **Accessibility** -- AI technology and knowledge should be widely accessible

## Key Procurement Guidelines (Table 7)

### 4.1 Approach to eliminate or minimize bias and ethical prejudices
- What training methods are used? Do they introduce fairness, interpretability, privacy, and security?
- What data is used for training? Has the model been trained on representative and complete datasets?
- Does the model come with model cards describing its nature and training data?
- Does the supplier use open-source ethical tooling to assess bias?
- Can the supplier identify possible sources of bias or unfairness?
- Does the supplier use any metrics (e.g., equalized odds or disparate impact) to evaluate bias?
- Has the supplier monitored for proxy variables (e.g., postal code as proxy for socioeconomic status)?
- Does the supplier use design ethics techniques (e.g., consequences scanning)?
- Does the solution address emerging risks such as misinformation, over-reliance, and loss of skills?

### 4.2 Process for ensuring ethical mindset in development team
- Does the AI/ML team take responsibility for how their work will be used?
- What ethical standards are encoded into the solution?
- Does the supplier offer ethics training or awareness programmes?
- Have there been past instances where AI did not meet performance requirements? What corrective measures were taken?
- Is there a redressal mechanism if individuals are affected negatively?
- Does the supplier consider user accessibility needs during design thinking?

### 4.3 Supplier's approach toward ethics at an organizational level
- Does the supplier's code of conduct highlight ethical use of digital technology?
- Does the supplier employ a chief AI ethics officer?
- Has senior leadership endorsed AI ethics publicly?
- Does the supplier have an AI ethics ambassador network?
- Does the supplier have a diversity, equity, and inclusion policy?
- Does the supplier use an AI ethics impact assessment?
- Does the supplier have an AI ethics board with enforcement power?
- Does the supplier have a whistle-blower protection policy?
- Does the supplier work with law enforcement/military agencies? What type of AI is being supplied?
- Does the supplier provide responsible use guidelines and a glossary of terms for ethics?

### 4.4 Environmental impact concerns
- What is the supplier's monthly average energy consumption (kWh)?
- How much energy is spent monthly for training AI solutions?
- What measures has the supplier adopted to optimize energy consumption?
- Does the supplier have a road map to become energy efficient in 3-5 years?
- Is the hardware infrastructure optimized to reduce energy consumption?
- Does the supplier use renewable energy sources? What is the percentage breakdown?
- Has the supplier considered possible environmental harm during data collection?

## Practical Takeaways for University Leaders

- **Bias in AI tools used for student-facing decisions is an existential risk for universities** -- Admissions algorithms, plagiarism detection, proctoring software, and retention prediction models all carry significant bias potential. Demand evidence of bias testing and mitigation
- **Require model cards** from vendors -- a standardized disclosure document describing what the model does, how it was trained, and its known limitations
- **Ask whether the vendor has a chief AI ethics officer and an ethics board with enforcement power** -- not just an advisory role
- **Environmental sustainability of AI should matter to universities** with climate commitments. Ask about energy consumption, renewable energy use, and hardware efficiency
- **Establish an institutional AI ethics review process** that mirrors IRB (Institutional Review Board) processes -- vet AI systems before deployment, not after problems emerge
- **Demand redressal mechanisms** -- if a student or employee is negatively affected by an AI decision, there must be a clear appeals process
- The seven core values (common good, transparency, equitability, accountability, inclusiveness, non-maleficence, accessibility) provide an excellent framework for a university AI ethics policy
