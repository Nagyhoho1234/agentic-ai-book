# Introduction

## Summary

The introduction traces the evolution of enterprise AI from rule-based expert systems in the early days of computing, through ML algorithms in the 1990s, to modern deep learning and generative AI. It establishes two primary enterprise use cases for AI: **automation** (data entry, customer service, inventory management) and **decision-making** (pricing optimization, business opportunity identification, investment decisions).

The section argues that responsible AI procurement guidelines are urgently needed because AI/ML adoption is skyrocketing post-COVID-19, the global AI market is projected to reach $1,500 billion by 2030, and AI solutions are now deeply embedded in critical enterprise applications influencing decisions. Yet in-house AI expertise remains limited and there is no established playbook.

### Key factors enterprises must consider when adopting AI:

1. **Business strategy alignment** -- Does the AI strategy align with corporate strategy?
2. **Business case** -- Is the investment justified? What is the long-term ROI?
3. **Technology and data integration** -- Does the solution meet data protection requirements?
4. **Ethics alignment** -- Is the provider aligned with organizational values?
5. **Risk assessment** -- How do you ensure ethical, equitable, and sustainable deployment?
6. **Agile and collaborative AI system integration** -- Is the system scalable and integrable?

### Scope

The guidelines focus wholly on **procuring AI software applications** (not hardware, robotics, or physical tools).

## Key Procurement Guidelines

- Guidelines are **not** intended to be a lift-and-shift solution, a MECE supplier questionnaire, or a hardware identification tool
- They **are** intended to provide a holistic assessment framework, serve as a general industry-agnostic guidebook, enable balanced decision-making (business needs vs. social/ethical responsibility), and provide a collaboration framework for stakeholders
- Procurement is best positioned to serve as the central coordination point, weaving together IT infrastructure integration, data gathering, stakeholder needs, project strategy, risk factors, and system implementation

### How to use the guidelines

- Consider proportionality -- not all guidelines apply to every procurement decision
- Different use cases will determine the importance of each consideration
- The guidelines are meant to be followed only after determining AI is the best solution for the specific problem

## Cross-Functional Collaboration (Table 1)

The report identifies five teams that should collaborate, led by procurement:

| Team | Key Responsibilities |
|------|---------------------|
| **Business/end user** | Use case prioritization, process definitions, workflow design, business requirements |
| **IT and cybersecurity** | Architecture design, infrastructure assessment, security, application management |
| **AI centre of excellence/data team** | AI capability assessment, tool configuration, process modelling, testing, ethics |
| **Data management** | Data needs, governance framework, data integrity requirements |
| **Procurement** | Align with business, collaborate with IT/data teams, assess market, coordinate implementation |

5. Contracting and implementation

## Practical Takeaways for University Leaders

- Universities should establish cross-functional AI evaluation teams mirroring the five-team model: faculty/academic users, IT/cybersecurity, institutional research/data science, data governance, and procurement
- Before procuring any AI tool, first confirm that AI is genuinely the best solution -- a non-AI alternative may suffice
- Procurement offices should embed responsible AI questions into their standard RFP/RFI templates
- The sourcing process framework (scoping, market intelligence, execution, evaluation, contracting) maps well to university procurement cycles
