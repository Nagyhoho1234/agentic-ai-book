# Chapter 1: Introduction

**Book:** *AI Snake Oil: What Artificial Intelligence Can Do, What It Can't, and How to Tell the Difference*
**Authors:** Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor (Princeton University)
**Published:** 2024

## Summary

The book opens with the explosive arrival of ChatGPT in November 2022, which became the fastest-growing consumer application in history, reaching 100 million users in two months. The authors use this moment to frame their central thesis: AI is an umbrella term covering radically different technologies with vastly different levels of reliability, and the public is poorly equipped to distinguish between what works and what is snake oil.

The authors identify three broad categories of AI:

1. **Generative AI** (e.g., ChatGPT, image generators) -- produces text, images, code, and other content. This category has genuine capabilities but also serious limitations including hallucination, copyright concerns, and potential for misuse.

2. **Predictive AI** -- attempts to forecast future events or classify data (e.g., recidivism prediction, hiring algorithms, medical diagnosis). This is where the most egregious snake oil is found, because many social outcomes are fundamentally unpredictable.

3. **Content moderation AI** -- used by social media platforms to police speech. The authors argue this is largely a failure driven more by institutional and political factors than by technical limitations.

The introduction establishes that AI is not one thing. Some applications genuinely work (spam filters, speech recognition, language translation), while others are fundamentally broken (predicting criminal behavior, forecasting job performance from video interviews). The key skill the book aims to teach is how to tell the difference.

The authors note that AI hype is driven by a confluence of corporate profit motives, uncritical journalism, researcher incentives, and cognitive biases that make the public susceptible to overblown claims.

## Key Definitions

- **AI Snake Oil:** AI products or applications that are sold with exaggerated or false claims about their capabilities, analogous to the fraudulent patent medicines of the 19th century.
- **Generative AI:** AI systems that generate new content (text, images, audio, video) rather than classifying or predicting.
- **Predictive AI:** AI systems that attempt to forecast outcomes or classify inputs based on patterns in historical data.
- **Content Moderation AI:** Automated systems used by platforms to identify and act on content that violates policies.
- **Automation Bias:** The human tendency to over-rely on automated systems, even when they produce incorrect results.

## Practical Takeaways for Scientists

- AI is not a monolithic technology. When evaluating an AI tool for research, first determine which category it falls into and what the established reliability is for that category.
- Generative AI tools can be useful research assistants but produce confident-sounding falsehoods (hallucinations). Always verify outputs.
- Be deeply skeptical of predictive AI tools that claim to forecast complex social or biological outcomes, especially when the vendor does not provide peer-reviewed evidence.
- The most important question about any AI tool is not "how impressive is the demo?" but "how does it perform on your specific real-world task?"
- AI-generated content is flooding the internet (estimated 90% of online content may be AI-generated by the mid-2020s), which has implications for data quality in research.
