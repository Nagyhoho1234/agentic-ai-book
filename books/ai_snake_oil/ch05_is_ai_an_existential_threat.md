# Chapter 5: Is AI an Existential Threat?

## Summary

This chapter examines the debate over whether AI poses an existential risk to humanity -- the idea that superintelligent AI could destroy or subjugate human civilization. The authors take a nuanced position: while they agree that long-term AI safety research is worthwhile, they argue that the current focus on speculative existential risk is disproportionate and diverts attention from real, present-day AI harms.

### The Existential Risk Narrative

The chapter traces the intellectual history of AI existential risk concerns, from early thinkers like I.J. Good (who coined the concept of an "intelligence explosion" in 1965) through modern advocates like Nick Bostrom, Eliezer Yudkowsky, and organizations like the Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI) and the Future of Life Institute. The core argument is that a sufficiently advanced AI might develop goals misaligned with human values and, being superintelligent, would be unstoppable.

### Why the Authors Are Skeptical

The authors identify several problems with the existential risk narrative:

1. **It conflates different types of AI.** Current AI systems are narrow tools, not general intelligences. The leap from "GPT-4 can pass the bar exam" to "AI will soon be smarter than all humans" ignores the vast gulf between narrow capabilities and general intelligence.

2. **It relies on untestable assumptions.** Claims about superintelligence are not falsifiable -- there is no way to verify or disprove predictions about systems that do not exist.

3. **It draws resources from present harms.** While billions are spent on speculative existential risk research, urgent problems like algorithmic bias, worker exploitation, and environmental costs receive far less attention and funding.

4. **It benefits AI companies.** The existential risk narrative positions AI as immensely powerful, which serves the marketing interests of companies selling AI products. Even criticism that frames AI as dangerous reinforces the perception that the technology is potent.

### The Concept of "Criti-hype"

The authors introduce the concept of "criti-hype" (coined by researcher Lee Vinsel): criticism that inadvertently hypes the technology it critiques. The open letter calling for a six-month pause on AI development beyond GPT-4 is cited as a prime example -- it ostensibly warned of dangers but simultaneously accepted at face value the claim that AI systems are "now becoming human-competitive at general tasks."

### Real Present-Day Harms

In contrast to speculative existential risks, the chapter catalogs documented, ongoing AI harms:
- Algorithmic bias in criminal justice, hiring, and healthcare
- Environmental costs of training large models
- Exploitation of low-wage data laborers who annotate training data
- Copyright infringement through training on artists' and authors' work
- Displacement of workers without adequate safety nets

### AGI and the Goalposts

The authors note that the definition of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) keeps shifting. Tasks that were once considered hallmarks of intelligence (chess, Go, language generation) are no longer seen as sufficient once AI achieves them. This moving goalpost makes AGI claims unfalsifiable and therefore unscientific.

## Key Definitions

- **Existential Risk (X-risk):** The hypothetical risk that AI could cause human extinction or permanent civilizational collapse.
- **Superintelligence:** A hypothetical AI system that surpasses human intelligence across all domains.
- **Intelligence Explosion:** The hypothetical scenario where an AI system recursively improves itself, rapidly becoming superintelligent.
- **Alignment Problem:** The challenge of ensuring that AI systems' goals remain aligned with human values.
- **Criti-hype:** Criticism of a technology that inadvertently reinforces hype about its power and importance.
- **AGI (Artificial General Intelligence):** A hypothetical AI system with human-level (or greater) capability across all cognitive domains.

## Practical Takeaways for Scientists

- When evaluating AI risk claims, distinguish between speculative risks (AI takeover, superintelligence) and documented risks (bias, inaccuracy, exploitation). Both deserve attention but at different priority levels.
- Be cautious about AI risk narratives that serve the interests of the entities promoting them. Companies benefit from being seen as creating powerful, potentially dangerous technology.
- The concept of criti-hype is a useful analytical tool: check whether your critique of an AI tool inadvertently amplifies its perceived power.
- Focus institutional AI governance efforts on concrete, measurable harms rather than speculative scenarios.
- The environmental costs of large model training are real and worth considering when choosing tools for research workflows. The energy consumption of a single large model training run can exceed the lifetime carbon footprint of a car.
