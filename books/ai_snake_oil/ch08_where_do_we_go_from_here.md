# Chapter 8: Where Do We Go From Here?

## Summary

The final chapter shifts from diagnosis to prescription, examining how society can shape AI development to serve the public interest rather than corporate profit. The authors draw analogies to the internet's evolution from publicly funded infrastructure to privately controlled oligopoly, and present two contrasting future scenarios through the stories of fictional children Kai and Maya.

### AI as Infrastructure

The authors compare AI's trajectory to the internet: both started as publicly funded research, both were privatized, and both risk being controlled by a few dominant companies. The early internet was funded by DARPA and developed with public resources, but today more than three-quarters of U.S. internet connectivity is controlled by just four companies (Comcast, Charter, Verizon, AT&T). AI research, once open and academic, is increasingly proprietary -- companies like OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic have stopped openly sharing research advances.

The authors argue this trajectory is not inevitable. Community networks (like Chattanooga, Tennessee's publicly owned gigabit network) demonstrate that public alternatives can outperform private ones.

### AI Snake Oil Appeals to Broken Institutions

A critical insight: the *demand* for AI snake oil comes from broken institutions. The authors argue that flawed organizations adopt dubious AI because:

- **Hiring is broken:** Overwhelmed HR departments use tools like HireVue because they have no good way to evaluate thousands of candidates.
- **Journalism is underfunded:** Revenue fell from $60 billion to $21 billion (2000-2022), leading outlets like CNET to adopt error-prone AI writing.
- **Education is underfunded:** Schools adopt AI plagiarism detectors (which are biased against non-native English speakers and unreliable) because teachers are overwhelmed.
- **Gun violence is unaddressed:** Schools spent $45 million on AI weapons detection (which flags lunchboxes as bombs) because the political system cannot address root causes.
- **Mental health care is inadequate:** Colleges adopted Social Sentinel (monitoring students' social media for self-harm) -- a tool so ineffective an employee called it "snake oil" internally -- instead of investing in counselors.

The pattern: AI is adopted as a cheap substitute for institutional reform, community investment, and adequate staffing.

### Embracing Randomness

The authors propose a radical alternative to predictive AI for resource allocation: **partial lotteries**. Instead of trying to predict who is the "best" applicant (a task at which AI consistently fails), establish a minimum threshold and then select randomly from the qualifying pool. Benefits include:

- Acknowledging inherent randomness in outcomes
- Reducing gaming and optimization pressure on applicants
- Countering rich-get-richer feedback loops
- Enabling rigorous evaluation of policy effects through natural experiments
- Reducing wasted time on application optimization

Applications discussed include college admissions, research grants, and hiring. The Oregon health insurance lottery (2008) is cited as a successful example of using randomization to both allocate resources and study policy effects.

### Regulation: Cutting Through the False Dichotomy

The authors reject both "regulation is futile" and "regulation is a panacea":

- **Existing frameworks work.** The U.S. already has vertical regulation (sector-specific agencies like the FDA, FTC, CFPB). The October 2023 executive order on AI delegated tasks to over 50 federal agencies. The EU has horizontal regulation including GDPR, the Digital Services Act, the Digital Markets Act, and the AI Act.
- **Enforcement matters more than new laws.** The FTC has used existing deceptive trade practices rules to act against AI companies making false claims.
- **Regulatory capture is a real risk.** Sam Altman's call for AI regulation included recommendations that would benefit OpenAI (licensing requirements that limit competition) while omitting transparency requirements that researchers had demanded.
- **Overzealous regulation can backfire.** The proposal to require licenses for training large AI models would concentrate power in existing large companies, similar to how Prohibition created black markets.

### AI and the Future of Work

The authors present a measured view of AI's labor impact:

- Claims of mass joblessness are overblown. Of hundreds of occupations in the 1950 U.S. census, only one (elevator operator) disappeared entirely due to automation.
- AI will change the nature of many jobs without eliminating them (the "automation paradox" -- ATMs reduced per-bank costs, leading to more banks and roughly the same number of tellers).
- The real concern is not that AI works too well but that employers will adopt it prematurely to cut costs, as NEDA did when it fired its eating disorder helpline workers and replaced them with a chatbot that recommended extreme calorie restriction.
- "Fears about technology are fears about capitalism" (Ted Chiang). Workers are not afraid of AI itself but of how employers will use it to reduce their power and agency.
- Proposed interventions: stronger unions, Universal Basic Income (Finland's experiment showed positive mental health effects without reducing work motivation), expanded unemployment insurance, and "robot taxes" to level the playing field between human and automated labor.

### Two Futures: Kai's World vs. Maya's World

The chapter concludes with two contrasting scenarios for a child born in 2022:

**Kai's world (dystopian but likely):** AI companies dominate with regulatory capture. AI-generated content floods social media, which generates addictive content calculated to appeal to specific users. Schools use predictive AI to track students, creating self-fulfilling prophecies. AGI promises keep people passive. Academic research and journalism are dependent on industry funding. Big Tech molds public perception unchecked.

**Maya's world (hopeful but requires action):** AI apps for children are safe and educational, backed by research funding and enforcement. Teachers have autonomy to experiment with AI in education. Interoperability regulation has broken social media monopolies. Partial lotteries replace opaque admissions systems. Antitrust regulation, copyright reform, and labor protections ensure AI companies share wealth. Continued human creativity is valued over AI mimicry.

The authors conclude that as of 2024, Kai's world is more likely, but Maya's world is achievable with sustained public engagement, institutional reform, and political will. The path of least resistance is not a happy one.

## Key Definitions

- **Partial Lottery:** A decision-making approach where all applicants meeting a threshold are entered into a random selection, replacing attempts at optimization-based ranking.
- **Automation Paradox:** The phenomenon where automation reduces costs, increases demand, and sometimes leads to more (not fewer) jobs in a sector.
- **Regulatory Capture:** When regulatory agencies serve the interests of the companies they regulate rather than the public interest.
- **Robot Tax:** A proposed tax on companies that use automation to replace human workers, aimed at leveling incentives between human and automated labor.
- **Universal Basic Income (UBI):** A system providing a fixed monthly payment to all citizens regardless of employment status.
- **Interoperability:** The ability of different platforms and systems to work together, reducing lock-in effects and promoting competition.
- **Ghost Work:** The hidden human labor (data labeling, content moderation, quality checking) that makes AI systems possible.

## Practical Takeaways for Scientists

- **When your institution proposes adopting AI tools, ask:** Is this addressing a genuine problem, or is it a cheap substitute for adequate staffing, training, or structural reform? The broken institution pattern is pervasive.
- **Consider partial lotteries** for grant allocation, student admissions, or other resource-distribution decisions where predictive optimization is unreliable.
- **Support open science and open-source AI.** The shift toward proprietary models threatens reproducibility and independent evaluation.
- **Engage in AI governance** at your institution. Scientists have the expertise to evaluate vendor claims and push back against snake oil purchases.
- **Be aware of the labor implications.** If your work involves AI, consider whether training data was produced ethically and whether the tool's deployment might harm workers.
- **The analogy to the internet is instructive.** The internet was more innovative and equitable when it was publicly funded and open. AI may follow the same pattern of privatization and concentration unless there is active resistance.
- **Demand transparency** from AI vendors. If a company will not share how its model was trained, what data it uses, or how it was validated, treat its claims with extreme skepticism.
